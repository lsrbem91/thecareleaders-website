Co-producing your Local Offer: care leaver local offer trainingWrite the offer with the people it is for.

Training that helps leaving care staff and care leavers rebuild your local offer together. You leave with rewritten sections of your offer and a review cycle.

The problem it solves

From 30 September 2026 the local offer has to say more. That includes accommodation and homelessness prevention for care leavers under 25, financial support, and what every department provides. Councils must consult care leavers. The guidance expects the offer to be co-produced and reviewed at least every two years. It has to work on a phone, not only as a PDF. An offer written by the leaving care team alone rarely meets that bar, and care leavers can tell.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, people will be able to:

  1. 1

    Describe, from a care leaver’s side, what it takes to find and use support in your current offer, and the points where they give up.

  2. 2

    Recognise when care leavers are being consulted on decisions already made rather than shaping them, and what that does to their trust.

  3. 3

    Explain what section 2 of the Children and Social Work Act 2017, as amended by the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026, and the September 2026 guidance require a local offer to include, separating statutory entitlements from discretionary support.

  4. 4

    Explain, using Laura Lundy’s model of participation (2007), the difference between consulting care leavers and co-producing with them, and plan how care leavers are recruited, paid, supported and kept safe, including groups who are often missed, such as young parents, care leavers in custody and former unaccompanied asylum-seeking children.

  5. 5

    Audit your current offer against the guidance, then rewrite sections of it in clear, everyday language with care leavers and test them on a phone.

  6. 6

    Design a review cycle, at least every two years, with “you said, we did” feedback to care leavers and oversight from the corporate parenting board.

Our learning framework in this course

Every Care Leaders Learning course follows Story, Theory, Action. Here is how it runs through Co-producing your Care Leaver Local Offer.

Story

Every part opens with a care leaver trying to use the offer.

Paige, 20, searches a 40-page PDF on her phone for help with a deposit. Rio, 19, gives up a shift for a consultation and never hears back.

Theory

Then the law or the model that explains what went wrong.

The September 2026 guidance and the corporate parenting principles in the Children and Social Work Act 2017. Laura Lundy’s model of participation, Roger Hart’s ladder and John Sweller’s cognitive load theory.

Action

Then a piece of your new offer, written together.

An audit checklist set against your current offer, and a co-production plan with roles and payment. Rewritten draft sections, and a review cycle with a “you said, we did” template.

The course in four parts

Each part opens with a care leaver’s story and ends with work staff and care leavers do together. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1The duty

Story. Paige, 20, needs help with a deposit for her first flat. She searches the council website on her phone and finds a 40-page PDF. Housing is on page 31, and it says support “may be available”. She gives up and borrows the money.

Theory. What the law and the September 2026 guidance require: the new content on accommodation, homelessness prevention and financial support and literacy, the age range up to 25, what every department provides, and publication care leavers can actually use. We link it to the corporate parenting principles in section 1 of the Children and Social Work Act 2017, including taking account of care leavers’ views, wishes and feelings.

Action. Staff and care leavers audit your current offer against a checklist built from the guidance. Each requirement is marked as met, partly met or missing, and care leavers say whether they could find it on a phone in under two minutes.

By the end of this part people can say what the guidance requires a local offer to include, and name the gaps in their own.

You leave with A checklist of what the guidance requires, set against your current offer.

2The people

Story. Rio, 19, is invited to a consultation event about the local offer. The draft has already been signed off. He gives up an afternoon’s shift to be there, and he never hears what happened to what he said.

Theory. Laura Lundy’s model of participation (2007) says young people need space to speak, support to give their voice, an audience who will listen and real influence on the decision. Roger Hart’s ladder of participation (1992) helps a group see the difference between tokenism and shared decisions. Rio got space and voice, and nothing else.

Action. Staff and care leavers plan co-production for your council using Lundy’s four elements as headings: who takes part, how they are paid and supported, how they are kept safe, who the decision makers are and how care leavers will see the result. They name the groups who rarely take part, such as young parents, care leavers in custody and former unaccompanied asylum-seeking children, and how to reach them.

By the end of this part people can explain the difference between consulting care leavers and co-producing with them, and agree a co-production plan with roles, payment and support.

You leave with A co-production plan for your council, with roles, payment and support agreed.

3The offer

Story. Back to Paige’s PDF. Her personal adviser tells her she is entitled to help with the deposit. The offer says “discretionary support may be available, subject to assessment”. Paige can’t tell which one is true.

Theory. The difference between statutory entitlements, which a care leaver can insist on, and discretionary support, which depends on the council. We add John Sweller’s cognitive load theory (1988): when someone is stressed and reading on a phone, every extra word and heading makes it harder to find the one thing they need.

Action. Language that Cares. Mixed tables of staff and care leavers take one section each, covering accommodation, money, education, training and employment, health and wellbeing, and relationships. They mark each item statutory or discretionary, rewrite it in clear, everyday language with who to contact, and test it on a phone with a care leaver who hasn’t seen it.

By the end of this part people can tell statutory from discretionary support in their own offer, and rewrite a section so a care leaver can understand and act on it from a phone.

You leave with A local offer checklist and rewritten draft sections to test with more care leavers.

4The review

Story. A year later Rio is asked to another consultation. His first question is “did anything change last time?” Nobody in the room knows.

Theory. Influence is the part of Lundy’s model most often missed: care leavers need to see what changed because they spoke up, and what didn’t and why. The guidance expects the offer to be reviewed at least every two years, and the corporate parenting board and scrutiny can hold each department to what it promised.

Action. Groups set a review cycle against your corporate parenting board’s calendar, name an owner in each department, and draft the first “you said, we did” update to care leavers from the changes made during the day.

By the end of this part people can set a review cycle with care leavers built in, and write a “you said, we did” update that tells care leavers what changed and what didn’t.

You leave with A review cycle with care leavers built in, and a “you said, we did” reporting template.

What changes

  • Involve care leavers as paid partners in writing and reviewing the offer, with support to take part safely.
  • Get named commitments from housing, finance, employment, health and leisure colleagues, not just children’s services.
  • Separate statutory entitlements from discretionary support, so care leavers know what they can insist on.
  • Publish the offer as plain-English web pages that work on a phone.
  • Report back to care leavers and the corporate parenting board on what changed and why.
  • An offer they helped write, in words they understand.
  • Knowing what each department offers them up to 25, and how to ask for it.
  • Hearing back about what changed because they spoke up, and what did not and why.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the day structured?

Four parts, each built the same way. Story: a care leaver, such as Paige, 20, searching for help with a deposit on her phone, or Rio, 19, who gave his views and never heard back. Theory: what explains it, from the law and the September 2026 guidance to Laura Lundy’s model of participation. Action: staff and care leavers work on your own offer together, auditing it, planning co-production, rewriting sections and setting the review cycle. You finish the day with draft sections of the offer, not just notes.

Should care leavers attend the training?

Yes. The course is designed for council staff and care leavers in the same room. We help you plan how care leavers are invited, paid and supported, so taking part is safe and worth their time.

What changed in September 2026?

Section 8 of the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 amended the local offer duty from 30 September 2026. The offer must now cover the arrangements for supporting care leavers, accommodation and homelessness prevention for care leavers under 25, and financial support and financial literacy. Updated statutory guidance came into force on the same date.

We already have a local offer. How often do we have to review it?

The guidance expects a review at least every two years, with further consultation. If you already have an offer, we start from it, test it against the new guidance and with care leavers, then plan the changes. We help you set a cycle that fits your corporate parenting board’s calendar and keeps care leavers informed in between.

How do you price it?

Each format has one flat fee. Face to face covers up to 60 people, and over 60 we charge for an additional facilitator. Live online covers up to 200 people. The workbook, the online course and the recording are included in the flat fee. Tell us what you need and we will send you a quote.

Who delivers it?

People with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services. Every course is designed by Luke Rodgers BEM and follows the same Story, Theory, Action approach.
