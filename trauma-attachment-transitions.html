Understanding care

Every move costs a child something.

For social workers, carers, residential staff and school staff who support children through moves between homes and schools. Your staff learn what developmental trauma and disrupted attachment mean for a child facing a move, and how to plan each move with the child, not around them. Children arrive somewhere new with more of their life, and more of their people, coming with them.

Length
6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm. 2 hours live online
How
Face to face or live online
Group
Up to 60 people
For
Social care, fostering, residential and school staff

The problem it solves

Moves between homes and schools are when placements wobble, attendance drops and behaviour spikes. A move is often arranged around a bed coming free or the end of a term, with little time to prepare the child, and the adults miss the old losses it brings back. The details that help a child settle, from routines to the people who matter, get lost on the way. This course follows one move from start to finish. Each part starts with a moment from the child’s side, explains it with the research, and ends with a tool your team uses on the next move it plans.

Helps withTrauma-informed practiceStability and matching

What changes

  • Plan moves with the child, asking what would help them feel safe and what they want to take with them.
  • Read behaviour before and after a move as a response to loss, not a reason to end the next home.
  • Arrange the new school place at the same time as the new home, with the virtual school.
  • Pass on the small details that help a child settle: routines, food, comforts and the people who matter.
  • Plan endings with key adults, including proper goodbyes, instead of letting relationships just stop.
  • Being asked about a move, not just told about it
  • Saying goodbye properly to people who matter
  • Familiar things and routines that move with them
  • A new home and school that already know something about them
  • Adults who expect the first weeks to be hard and stay patient

Story

  • Describe what a move feels like from a child’s side, in the weeks before it, on the day and in the first week.
  • Recognise the signs that a move is bringing back earlier losses, before and after it happens.

Theory

  • Explain what developmental trauma is and isn’t, and how early experiences of threat affect a child’s stress response, learning and behaviour.
  • Explain, using Bowlby’s and Ainsworth’s attachment research and Pauline Boss’s idea of ambiguous loss, why some children test every new adult and why moves can reopen old losses.

Action

  • Plan a move with the child using a transition planning tool, covering goodbyes, what goes with them, the school place and the first week, in line with the statutory guidance on education stability.
  • Write a short handover for the new carers and school about what helps the child settle.
  • Social workers, personal advisers and independent reviewing officers
  • Foster carers, supervising social workers and residential staff
  • Designated teachers, pastoral staff and virtual school staff
  • Health and early help staff who stay with a child through a move
  • Up to 60 people, ideally a mix of social care and school staff. No prior training is needed.

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Each part opens with a moment from one child’s move, explains it with the research and ends with a tool your team practises in the room. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1Trauma

Story. Leah, 11, is told on a Tuesday that she is moving on Friday. She packs her own things into bin bags, says nothing at school and is excluded on Thursday for throwing a chair.

Theory. Developmental trauma: what happens to a developing brain and body when the world has not been safe, and how it shows up in stress, memory and behaviour. We explain where the term comes from, Bessel van der Kolk’s proposal of developmental trauma disorder (2005), and what it isn’t: it is not a recognised diagnosis in DSM-5 or ICD-11, and it is not a label for a child.

Action. Staff read Leah’s week back from her side and mark where the threat she felt shows up in what she did. Then, in pairs, they write a plain-English explanation of developmental trauma they could give a carer or a teacher in two minutes, and test it on each other.

By the end of this part people can explain developmental trauma in plain words to a carer or teacher, and say what it is and isn’t.

You leave with A shared, plain-English explanation of developmental trauma the whole team can use.

2Attachment

Story. Three weeks into her new home, Leah breaks her carer’s favourite mug, then watches to see what happens. She does it again a week later.

Theory. Bowlby’s attachment theory (1969) and Mary Ainsworth’s research on patterns of attachment (1978) explain how early relationships shape whether a child expects adults to stay, and why a child may test a new carer to find out. The testing is a question: are you going to send me away too?

Action. PACE. Staff map how a child they know tests new adults and what each test is checking, then practise PACE responses (playfulness, acceptance, curiosity and empathy) to Leah’s mug and to their own child’s tests.

By the end of this part people can describe what a child’s testing behaviour might be checking, and respond to it with PACE instead of a sanction.

You leave with A way to read testing behaviour through attachment, and PACE responses that help the child trust you.

3Transitions

Story. Sam, 15, is moving homes in the spring of Year 11. Nobody has asked him about his school, his football team or his younger brother, who is staying where he is.

Theory. Pauline Boss’s ambiguous loss (1999) explains grief for people who are still alive but no longer there, which is what many moves in care feel like. We then cover what the rules expect: the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010 and the statutory guidance on promoting the education of looked-after and previously looked-after children, including a school place arranged alongside the new home and a nominated officer approving any move that would disrupt education in Years 10 and 11.

Action. Staff draw a timeline of one child’s moves and losses, anonymised, and mark where the next move will echo an earlier one. They then check a planned move against the statutory expectations on education.

By the end of this part people can spot where a move is likely to hurt most for a child, and name what the regulations and education guidance require before a move goes ahead.

You leave with Prompts for spotting where a move will hurt most, and what to put in place.

4Planning well

Story. Sam’s move happens again, this time planned with him. He chooses who he says goodbye to, his football kit goes in the car, and his new school knows his name before he arrives.

Theory. Gillian Schofield and Mary Beek’s Secure Base model, developed at the University of East Anglia, sets out what carers do that helps a child feel safe: being available, sensitive, accepting, encouraging co-operation and making the child part of the family. We use it to plan the first week.

Action. Staff use a transition planning tool on a move their team is supporting, planned with the child: who they need to say goodbye to, what goes with them, what the new home and school need to know and how the first week will go. They finish by writing the one-page handover.

By the end of this part people can complete a transition plan with a child and write a one-page handover for the new home and school.

You leave with A transition planning tool your team can use straight away, tried on a real move.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the session structured?

Four parts that follow one move from start to finish. Story: a moment from the child’s side, such as Leah being told on a Tuesday that she moves on Friday. Theory: the research that explains it, from developmental trauma and attachment to ambiguous loss and the Secure Base model, plus the statutory rules on moves and education. Action: a tool staff practise in the room, ending with a transition plan and handover for a real move. It runs for 6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm, with breaks. Live online it is a 2-hour session covering the key story, theory and tool from each part.

Is it for schools or social care?

Both. A move hits a child at home and at school at the same time, so the course works with social workers, school staff and carers in the same session, planning from the same tool.

Does it cover school moves as well as moves between homes?

Yes. We look at moves between homes and between schools, endings with key adults and new starts. The planning tool works for any of them.

How does it fit with the statutory guidance?

It works to the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010 and the statutory guidance on promoting the education of looked-after and previously looked-after children. That means a new school place arranged with the virtual school head at the same time as the new home, and any move that would disrupt education in key stage 4 approved by a nominated officer after consulting the designated teacher and the IRO.

Can you shape it around our service?

Yes. We use your own transition paperwork where you have it, and examples from your fostering, residential or school settings.

Who delivers it?

Trainers with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services. The course was designed by Luke Rodgers BEM, who grew up in care and has spent his career leading change in children’s services, and it follows our Story, Theory, Action approach.
