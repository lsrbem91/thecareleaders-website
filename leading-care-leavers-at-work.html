Leaving care and work

Leading Care Leavers at Work: training for managers and HRA first job can change everything.

For line managers, supervisors and HR or apprenticeship teams who employ care leavers. Managers learn why the first 12 weeks matter so much, how to give feedback and hold the standard without losing the person, and what to do when housing, money or family crises reach work. More care leavers stay, settle and progress.

Length
6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm. 2 hours live online
How
Face to face or live online
Group
Up to 60 people
For

The problem it solves

Care leavers can drop out of first jobs and apprenticeships in the early weeks, often for reasons their manager never sees: a housing problem, no family to fall back on for money, or a sharp reply that is really about something else. A late arrival turns into a disciplinary instead of a conversation. Employers who sign the Care Leaver Covenant’s Care Leaver Friendly Employer Charter commit to clear expectations, support such as a workplace buddy, and responding to challenges with empathy. This course shows managers how to do that day to day without lowering the bar. Each part starts with a moment at work seen from the young person’s side, explains what is going on underneath, and ends with a tool managers use with their team the next day.

Helps withCare leavers, work and the local offer

What changes

  • Plan an induction that makes a new starter feel expected from day one, with a named person to go to.
  • Give feedback and set boundaries in a way that holds the standard and keeps the person in the room.
  • Notice when housing, money or family pressures are affecting work, and have an early conversation.
  • Know where to point someone for help: their personal adviser, the council’s local offer for care leavers and, for apprentices, the care leavers’ bursary.
  • Keep the same standards while changing how support is offered.
  • A manager who understands why the first months can feel so hard.
  • Someone to go to when life outside work gets in the way.
  • Feedback that is straight and fair, given in private.
  • A fair chance to stay in work, progress and build a career.

Story

  • Describe what leaving care can mean for a young person aged 16 to 25 starting work, including having less family back-up for money, housing and advice.
  • Recognise early signs that a housing, money or family crisis is affecting someone’s work, and act before it becomes a disciplinary issue.

Theory

  • Explain, using Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance (1999), how past trauma can show up at work as lateness, shutting down or a sharp reaction to criticism.
  • Explain why belonging in the first 12 weeks matters, drawing on Baumeister and Leary’s need to belong (1995), and how Kim Scott’s Radical Candor (2017) helps a manager hold the standard and keep the relationship.

Action

  • Plan a 12-week induction with clear expectations, a named buddy and check-ins booked in advance, and use a feedback script that names the standard, asks what is going on and agrees the next step.
  • Point a care leaver employee to the right support, including their personal adviser up to age 25, the council’s local offer and the £3,000 care leavers’ bursary for apprentices who start under 25, and hold a progression conversation that agrees a realistic next step.
  • Line managers and supervisors of care leavers, including first-time managers
  • HR, recruitment and apprenticeship teams
  • Council departments employing care leavers, and businesses signed up to the Care Leaver Covenant
  • Workplace mentors and buddies
  • Up to 60 people per group. No knowledge of care or trauma is needed.

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Every part opens with a moment at work from the young person’s side, explains what is going on underneath and ends with a tool managers practise in the room. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1Understanding

Story. Jess, 18, starts an apprenticeship two weeks after moving into her first flat on her own. On day three her manager tells her, in front of the team, to tie her hair back. She walks out at lunch and does not come back that afternoon.

Theory. Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance (1999) explains why a small criticism can feel like a big threat to someone whose early life was not safe, and why they may fight, run or shut down rather than talk it through. Behaviour is communication: Jess’s walk-out is telling her manager something about how the comment landed, not that she does not want the job.

Action. Separating Reason from Behaviour. Managers take three everyday moments, lateness, silence and a sharp reply, write down what the person did, then list possible reasons from the young person’s side before deciding how to respond.

By the end of this part people can explain the window of tolerance in plain words and name at least two possible reasons behind a behaviour at work before they respond to it.

You leave with A one-page guide to what may sit behind lateness, silence or a sharp reply.

2Starting well

Story. Bailey, 19, does not ask where the staff kitchen is or when he can take a break. He spends his first week not eating at work, and his manager reads his quietness as not being interested.

Theory. Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary’s need to belong (1995) shows that feeling part of a group is a basic human need, not a nice extra. For someone who has moved homes many times, feeling expected on day one matters even more. The Care Leaver Friendly Employer Charter asks for clear expectations and support such as a workplace buddy, and we show what that looks like in your workplace.

Action. Managers build a 12-week induction plan: a named buddy, the unwritten rules written down, check-ins booked before problems start, and a private conversation where the young person chooses whether to tell their manager about their care experience.

By the end of this part people can plan the first 12 weeks for a new starter, including a named person, written expectations and booked check-ins.

You leave with A 12-week induction checklist.

3Staying well

Story. Kayla, 20, has been late three times this month. Her manager is about to start a disciplinary. What he does not know is that her housing benefit has stopped and she has been sleeping on a friend’s sofa across town.

Theory. Kim Scott’s Radical Candor (2017) says good feedback cares personally and challenges directly. Drop the care and it feels like an attack. Drop the challenge and the standard slips. Care leavers need both, from someone who asks what is going on before deciding what to do.

Action. Managers learn a three-step feedback script: name the standard, ask what is going on, agree the next step. They rehearse Kayla’s conversation in threes, then use Rewind and Replay to go back over a hard conversation of their own and try it again. Then they write a crisis plan with local contacts, including when and how to involve the young person’s personal adviser.

By the end of this part people can hold a feedback conversation that keeps the standard and the relationship, and know who to contact when a housing, money or family crisis reaches work.

You leave with A feedback script for hard conversations, and a crisis plan with local contacts.

4Growing

Story. Liam, 21, is offered a permanent job at the end of his apprenticeship. He nearly turns it down, because he does not believe he will be good enough and expects to be found out.

Theory. Carol Dweck’s work on growth mindset (Mindset, 2006) shows that people who believe ability can grow stick with hard things for longer. Managers can build that belief through the way they talk about mistakes and progress. We cover the practical support that sits behind progression: apprenticeships, the £3,000 care leavers’ bursary for apprentices who start under 25, and the council’s local offer.

Action. Growth Mindset. Managers practise a progression conversation that names what the person has already learned, agrees one realistic next step and sets a date to review it.

By the end of this part people can hold a progression conversation that agrees a realistic next step, and name the funding and local support available to a care leaver employee.

You leave with A plan for progression conversations with each care leaver you manage.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the day structured?

Four parts across a 6-hour day: understanding, starting well, staying well and growing. Each starts with a moment at work from the young person’s side, such as Jess walking out after being corrected in front of the team. Then the idea that explains it, from the window of tolerance to Radical Candor. Then a tool managers practise in the room and use the next day: Separating Reason from Behaviour, a 12-week induction plan, a feedback script and a progression conversation. Online, one 2-hour live session covers the core of each part, with the on-demand course to go deeper.

Do our managers need to know about care or trauma already?

No. The course starts from what leaving care and trauma can mean at work, then stays with the everyday decisions managers make: inductions, supervision, feedback and what to do when a crisis hits.

Does this mean lowering standards for care leavers?

No. It is about support without lowering the bar. Managers keep the same expectations and learn how to give the extra support that helps someone meet them.

Will managers know who is a care leaver?

Only if the young person chooses to tell them. The course covers how to make that easy and what changes when they do. For apprentices, the employer’s additional funding for a care leaver is only generated if the apprentice consents to their status being shared.

Does it fit with the Care Leaver Covenant?

Yes. The Care Leaver Friendly Employer Charter asks employers to give existing staff guidance on supporting care-experienced colleagues. This course is that guidance for your managers.
