Understanding care

Supporting Boys in Care: training for carers, schools and youth justiceBoys in care need to be seen too.

A 6-hour course for foster carers, residential staff, school pastoral teams and youth justice workers who support boys in care. Too many boys in care show distress as anger, and too often the adult response is a sanction, a police call or a move. You’ll learn why that happens, how to stay alongside a boy when he pushes you away, and how to agree a plan with the other adults that keeps him in his school and his home.

Length
6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm. 2 hours live online
How
Face to face or live online
Group
Up to 60 people
For
Foster carers, children’s home staff, school pastoral teams and youth justice workers

The problem it solves

The DfE’s figures for 2025 show boys make up 56% of children in care in England. Many have learned that anger is safer to show than fear or sadness, and adults often meet that anger with a sanction, a police call or a move. The government’s 2018 national protocol on reducing unnecessary criminalisation of looked-after children and care leavers says carers should deal with low-level incidents at home, as a reasonable parent would, and use restorative approaches first. Staff need the skills and the backing to do that. The day follows one pattern four times: a story from a boy’s side, the theory that explains what’s going on, and a tool you practise in the room and use the same week.

Helps withBehaviour, incidents and restraintSchool attendance and attainment

What changes

  • Read anger and withdrawal as communication, and respond to the feeling as well as the behaviour
  • Build trust side by side, in the car, over food or during an activity, not only across a table
  • Manage incidents at home without calling the police for things a reasonable parent would not report
  • Use restorative conversations after an incident so a boy can put things right and stay
  • Work to a team plan agreed with the other adults around the same boys, at home and in school
  • Adults who stay calm and stay around when they push them away
  • Conversations that happen while doing something, not interviews
  • Fewer police calls and moves for things that could be sorted at home
  • Someone who asks how they are and keeps asking

Story

  • Describe how one incident can turn into a police call, an exclusion and a move, from the boy’s side as well as the adults’.
  • Recognise the feelings, such as fear, shame and loss, that can sit underneath anger or withdrawal in a boy they support.

Theory

  • Explain, using Bowlby’s attachment theory (1969) and Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance (1999), why anger can feel safer to show than sadness or fear.
  • Apply the 2018 national protocol on reducing unnecessary criminalisation to decide when an incident can be managed without the police.

Action

  • Use PACE and side-by-side conversation during a shared activity to build trust with a boy who avoids talking face to face.
  • Plan a restorative conversation after an incident, and agree a team plan with named actions that keeps a boy in his school and his home.
  • Foster carers, supervising social workers and residential children’s home staff
  • Pastoral, behaviour and inclusion staff in schools, and designated teachers
  • Youth justice, early help and youth workers, and personal advisers
  • Up to 60 people. No prior training is needed, and mixed groups of care and school staff work well because the plan is shared

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Every part opens with a story from a boy’s side, gives you the theory that explains it and ends with a tool people practise in the room and use the next day. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1What we see

Story. Dylan, 15, kicks a hole in a door at his children’s home at 11pm, after the phone call his dad promised doesn’t happen. Staff call the police. By the end of the month he has been arrested, excluded from school for five days and moved to a home 40 miles away.

Theory. The 2018 national protocol on reducing unnecessary criminalisation of looked-after children and care leavers says the police should be a last resort for incidents a reasonable parent would manage at home. Behaviour is communication: the door is about the phone call, not the door.

Action. Separating Reason from Behaviour. Staff trace Dylan’s month, mark each point where a different response was possible, then hold their own last three incidents up against the same questions.

By the end of this part people can describe the chain from incident to police call to move, and name at least two points in their own service where it could be broken.

You leave with Questions to test how the incident to police to move pattern shows up in your service

2What is underneath

Story. Josh, 12, is asked by his foster carer whether he misses his mum. He says “I don’t care”, goes to his room and smashes his games controller.

Theory. Bowlby’s attachment theory (1969) explains why a boy who has lost the people closest to him may protect himself by showing nothing, or showing anger. Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance (1999) explains why a small question can tip him out of the zone where he can think. We also look at how ideas about being a man, picked up from family, friends and online, can make sadness feel unsafe to show.

Action. Window of Tolerance, and Spot, Soothe, Shift. Staff map the early signs for an anonymised boy they support, then practise spotting the shift, soothing first and shifting the conversation once he’s calmer.

By the end of this part people can explain in plain words why anger can feel safer than sadness, and map one boy’s early signs and what helps him come back.

You leave with A window of tolerance map for one boy, and the feeling to respond to underneath the behaviour

3What works

Story. Dylan’s keyworker starts driving him to football training. Looking at the road, not at her, Dylan says more in 20 minutes than in six months of sit-down keywork sessions.

Theory. Dan Hughes’s PACE (playfulness, acceptance, curiosity and empathy) gives adults a way to stay alongside a boy without lecturing or giving up. Restorative approaches, which the 2018 protocol expects to come first, let a boy put things right and stay, rather than be punished and moved.

Action. PACE and a restorative conversation. In threes, staff practise opening a conversation during an activity, such as driving, cooking or gaming, then plan the restorative conversation after Dylan’s door, using four questions: what happened, who was affected, how do you feel now, what do we do to put it right.

By the end of this part people can start a side-by-side conversation using PACE, and plan a restorative conversation after a real incident.

You leave with Conversation starters for alongside an activity, and a restorative conversation plan

4Our plan

Story. Josh’s carer, his head of year and his youth worker each have a different rule for when he walks out. He learns fast that the adults don’t talk to each other, and which one to push.

Theory. Haim Omer’s Non-Violent Resistance (2004) rests on adult presence: a network of adults who stay, don’t escalate, and act together so a child knows they won’t give up on him. That shared presence is what makes the other tools hold.

Action. A team plan. Carers, school and youth justice staff working with the same boys agree one shared response to the moments that go wrong, who does what, and how they’ll know it’s working.

By the end of this part people can agree a shared response with the other adults around a boy and write named actions with a review date.

You leave with A team action plan with named actions and a review date

Questions commissioners ask

How is the day structured?

Four parts, each built the same way. Story: a moment from a boy’s side, such as Dylan’s night with the police or Josh smashing his controller. Theory: the idea that explains it, from attachment and the window of tolerance to Haim Omer’s Non-Violent Resistance and the 2018 national protocol. Action: a tool people practise in the room, such as PACE, Spot, Soothe, Shift or a restorative conversation, and use the same week. It ends with a team plan. Face to face it runs for 6 hours, usually 10am to 4pm, with breaks. Live online it is a 2-hour session covering the key story, theory and tool from each part.

Does it cover youth justice?

Yes. Keeping boys out of the justice system runs through the course, including what the 2018 national protocol on reducing unnecessary criminalisation expects of carers, staff and police. Youth justice workers are one of the groups it was built for.

Can school and care staff do it together?

Yes, and it helps. The team plan in part 4 works best when the adults around the same boys agree it together, at home and in school.

Is it only relevant to boys?

The focus is boys because of the patterns we look at. Most of what people practise, such as side-by-side conversation, PACE and restorative approaches, works with girls too, and we say so in the room.

Can you shape it around our service?

Yes. We tailor the examples and the mix of people in the room to your service, your procedures and the children you work with. If you have a local agreement with the police on responding to incidents in children’s homes, we build it into part 1.

Who delivers it?

People with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services. Every course is designed by Luke Rodgers BEM and follows the same Story, Theory, Action approach.

How do you price it?

We quote per cohort, based on how many people, the format and how much tailoring you want. Tell us what you need and we’ll send you a clear quote.
