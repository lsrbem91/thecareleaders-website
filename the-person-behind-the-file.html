The Person Behind the File: training on care-experienced adultsThey were a child in care first.

Training that helps probation, prison, police, housing and health staff see the care experience behind the file. You leave with a checklist of changes for your next appointment.

The problem it solves

Many adults on probation, in custody or presenting as homeless spent part of their childhood in care. It rarely shows on the file unless someone asks. A missed appointment gets logged as non-compliance and a sharp reply as hostility. Since 30 September 2026 care leavers under 25 can no longer be found intentionally homeless. The HMPPS framework now asks prisons to identify care experience and, with consent, contact the local authority.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, people will be able to:

  1. 1

    Recognise signs that someone may have grown up in care when it is not on the file, and ask about it in a way that respects consent

  2. 2

    Describe a missed appointment or a sharp reply from the side of a young adult who grew up with many moves and adults who left

  3. 3

    Explain the difference between care experienced and care leaver, who is entitled to leaving care support and a personal adviser up to age 25, and what the HMPPS framework for prisoners with care experience and the change to intentional homelessness rules mean for your service

  4. 4

    Explain, using Bowlby’s attachment theory (1969) and Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance (1999), how early trauma, broken attachments and repeated moves can shape trust, memory and responses to authority in adulthood

  5. 5

    Apply the six principles in the government’s working definition of trauma-informed practice (2022) to plan changes to appointments, letters and first conversations, and read missed appointments this way before recording them as non-compliance

  6. 6

    Identify the right contacts in the leaving care team and the local offer, and plan when and how to involve the person’s personal adviser

Our learning framework in this course

Every Care Leaders Learning course follows Story, Theory, Action. Here is how it runs through The Person Behind the File.

Story

The course follows one young adult’s file, retold from his side.

Dean, 23, is on a community order. His 40-page file never mentions his eight years in care. Shay, 22, arrives at the housing options desk with one bag.

Theory

Then the theory or the rules that explain it.

The Children Act 1989 definitions and the HMPPS Supporting Prisoners with Care Experience Policy Framework. Bowlby’s attachment theory, Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance and the working definition of trauma-informed practice (2022).

Action

Then a change staff make at their next appointment.

Questions for asking about care experience, and a replay of one moment from your own caseload. A new way to read missed appointments, and a checklist of changes with local contacts.

The course in four parts

Each part opens with a story from the person’s side and ends with a tool for the next appointment. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1The file

Story. Dean, 23, is on a community order. His file runs to 40 pages: three missed appointments, two warnings and a note from induction that he was “evasive”. Nothing in it says he spent eight years in care, because nobody asked.

Theory. The definitions that decide entitlement. Care experienced means anyone who has been in care at any point. Care leaver is a legal status under the Children Act 1989, based on when and how long someone was looked after, and it brings leaving care support, including a personal adviser up to 25 if they want one. We then set out what the HMPPS Supporting Prisoners with Care Experience Policy Framework asks staff to do: identify care experience, record it with consent and, for people under 25, contact the local authority.

Action. Language that Cares. Staff read Dean’s record and mark what it says about him and what it leaves out. In pairs they practise asking about care in a first meeting, in plain words that do not make the person prove it, and agree how to record the answer with consent. They then rewrite one line of Dean’s record so it describes what happened without the label.

By the end of this part people can ask about care experience in a first meeting in a way that respects consent, and say whether the person is likely to be entitled to a personal adviser.

You leave with A short set of questions for asking about care experience, and a note on recording it with consent

2The person

Story. The same file, retold from Dean’s side. He went into care at 11 and moved nine times. Most moves started with a phone call or a letter he wasn’t expecting. At 18 his social worker changed, then his personal adviser, and he stopped answering numbers he didn’t know.

Theory. Bowlby’s attachment theory (1969): early relationships teach us whether adults will stay and can be trusted, and those expectations carry into adulthood. For someone whose adults kept leaving, ignoring a probation letter can be a way of not being let down again. We draw on our lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services to show what that looks like from the inside.

Action. Rewind and Replay. Groups take one moment from Dean’s file, rewind it and replay it from his side: what he saw, what he expected and what the record said. They then do the same with a moment from a case of their own, without names, swap with a partner and replay each other’s moment.

By the end of this part people can describe a missed appointment or a sharp reply from the person’s side, and name the past experience that might sit behind it.

You leave with Dean’s story, and a replay of one moment from your own caseload, to hold in mind the next time a file reads like non-compliance

3The pattern

Story. Dean is called in to discuss a breach. He waits 40 minutes, then hears the word “warning” in the first sentence. He shouts and walks out, and later can’t remember what he was told.

Theory. Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance (1999): under threat, people tip into fight, flight or shutdown and can’t take in or remember information. People who grew up with unpredictable adults can tip faster and at smaller things. We look at why letters, long waits and fixed slots set some people up to fail before they reach the room.

Action. Window of Tolerance and Separating Reason from Behaviour. Staff list reactions they see every week, such as shouting, walking out or leaving post unopened, then map each one against what it may be protecting the person from, and mark what in their own process tips people out of the window. In pairs they practise the opening of Dean’s breach meeting so it starts with him, not the warning.

By the end of this part people can explain the window of tolerance in plain words, and read a missed appointment or hostile reply in a way that points to what to try next.

You leave with A way to read a missed appointment or a hostile reply that points to what to try next

4The approach

Story. Shay, 22, arrives at the housing options desk with one bag. She left her flat after rent arrears and says she doesn’t need a social worker. She hasn’t seen her personal adviser in months and isn’t sure she still has one.

Theory. The Office for Health Improvement and Disparities’ working definition of trauma-informed practice (2022) sets out six principles: safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, empowerment and cultural consideration. We turn them into front-desk practice, then cover the support around the person: the local offer, the leaving care team and, since 30 September 2026, the rule that care leavers under 25 cannot be found intentionally homeless.

Action. Groups bring a case from their own caseload, without names, and use the six principles as a checklist to plan changes: a text reminder, a plain-English letter, a named worker, a first call to the leaving care team with the person’s consent. Groups share their plans and test each one against the six principles. They finish by finding their own council’s local offer and leaving care contacts.

By the end of this part people can plan specific changes to appointments, letters and first conversations for one person on their caseload, and say when and how they will involve the personal adviser and leaving care team.

You leave with A checklist of changes and local contacts to use at your next appointment

What changes

  • Ask about care experience early and sensitively, and record it with the person’s consent.
  • Treat a missed appointment as a prompt to follow up, with a call, a reminder or a different time, not as a refusal.
  • Explain decisions plainly, and make promises that are small enough to keep.
  • Check whether the person has a personal adviser and invite them into sentence, release or housing planning.
  • Use the local authority’s local offer for care leavers to find what the person is entitled to.
  • Being asked about their history once, kindly, and not having to prove it.
  • Staff who follow up when they miss an appointment instead of closing the case.
  • A personal adviser who knows where they are and is part of the plan.
  • Housing, release and health plans that use the support they are entitled to.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the session structured?

Face to face it is a 6-hour day in four parts. Online it is one 2-hour live session covering the core of each part. Each part is built the same way. Story: Dean, 23, whose probation file says “evasive” and never mentions his eight years in care, and Shay, 22, at a housing desk. Theory: the idea or the rules that explain what staff are seeing, from attachment theory and the window of tolerance to the HMPPS framework and the intentional homelessness change. Action: a tool staff practise in the room, such as asking about care in a first meeting or replaying a moment from the person’s side, and use at their next appointment. The last part is spent planning changes for a case from your own caseload.

Does it fit the HMPPS framework for prisoners with care experience?

Yes. The prisons version is built around the Supporting Prisoners with Care Experience Policy Framework, issued in May 2026: identifying care experience, recording it with consent, contacting the local authority for people under 25 and involving personal advisers in sentence and release planning. It suits senior leads, single points of contact, keyworkers and prison offender managers, and sits alongside the HMPPS care experience eLearning rather than replacing it.

We work in probation, not prisons. Is it relevant?

Yes. The Ministry of Justice and HMPPS guidance Care experience matters (2023) sets out how probation, prisons and local authorities can work together for people with care experience, in custody, on release and under community supervision. We use probation examples: missed appointments, enforcement decisions and release planning.

We are a housing team. Is this relevant?

Yes. Housing staff are often the first service to meet a care leaver in crisis. Since 30 September 2026, care leavers under 25 can no longer be found intentionally homeless under the Housing Act 1996. We cover what that changes at the front desk and how to work with the leaving care team and the personal adviser.

Who delivers it?

Trainers with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services. The course was designed by Luke Rodgers BEM, who grew up in care and has spent his career leading change in children’s services, and it follows our Story, Theory, Action approach.

Can you shape it around our service, and how do you price it?

Yes. We use examples from your setting, whether that is a custody suite, a probation office, a housing options desk or a community mental health team, and build in your local leaving care contacts and local offer. Each format has one flat fee. Face to face covers up to 60 people, and over 60 we charge for an additional facilitator. Live online covers up to 200 people. The workbook, the online course and the recording are included in the flat fee. Tell us what you need and we will send you a quote.
