Voice

Voice is only worth it if it changes something.

For participation workers, children in care council leads and the managers who decide what happens to young people’s views. Both groups learn the Lundy model together and map the route from a session with young people to a named decision. Young people hear what changed, what did not, and why, and they keep coming back.

Length
6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm. 2 hours live online
How
Face to face or live online
Group
Up to 60 people
For
Participation workers, children in care council leads and managers

The problem it solves

Most services run consultations, children in care councils and surveys, then struggle to name a decision that changed because of them. Young people notice when nothing comes back and stop turning up. Asking for views and doing nothing with them does more harm than not asking. The corporate parenting principles require councils to take children’s views into account, and Ofsted’s care leavers judgement looks at young people’s ability to influence the services they receive. A count of sessions shows neither. Every part of this course opens with a young person’s experience of having their say, gives you the model that explains where it went wrong, and ends with a tool for making the next piece of work count.

Helps withChildren’s voice and participationGetting ready for inspection

What changes

  • Agree the decision, and who will make it, before planning any session with young people
  • Check each piece of participation work gives young people space, support to speak, a real audience and influence
  • Take young people’s views to the manager who decides, by an agreed route and timescale
  • Tell young people what changed, what did not, and why, by an agreed date
  • Know before they take part what a session can and cannot change
  • See what changed because they spoke up
  • Get an honest answer when something cannot change
  • Are kept safe, prepared and paid fairly when they take part

Story

  • Describe what giving their views and never hearing back is like from a young person’s side, and why they stop taking part.
  • Identify where young people’s views in your own service stop before they reach the person who decides.

Theory

  • Apply Laura Lundy’s model of participation (2007), space, voice, audience and influence, to test a piece of participation work in your own service.
  • Explain the difference between consultation, participation and co-production using Roger Hart’s ladder of participation (1992), and describe what the corporate parenting principles expect on children’s views.

Action

  • Plan a participation activity around a named decision, a named decision-maker and a date for feedback, with the preparation, consent, safeguarding and payment young people need.
  • Write a you said, we did, and why not response young people can understand, and show a corporate parenting board or inspector what difference their views made.
  • Participation and youth voice workers
  • Children in care council and care leaver forum leads
  • Team managers, service managers and heads of service who receive young people’s views
  • Independent reviewing officers and virtual school staff who gather children’s views
  • Up to 60 people, with no prior training needed. It works best when participation staff and the managers who act on their work come together

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Every part opens with a young person’s experience of having their say, gives you the model that explains it, and ends with a tool your team practises on its own live work. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1Why it fails

Story. Tegan, 15, spent an evening at her children in care council designing a welcome pack for children coming into care. Eight months later she has heard nothing about it. She doesn’t go back.

Theory. Roger Hart’s ladder of participation (1992) separates real participation from decoration and tokenism. Laura Lundy’s model (2007), built on Article 12 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, says children’s views need space, voice, an audience and influence. Tegan had the first two. Nobody planned the last two.

Action. The Lundy check. Groups take one live piece of participation work from their own service, test it against the four parts and mark where it is strong and where it stops.

By the end of this part people can explain consultation, participation and co-production with an example of each, and test a piece of work against Lundy’s four parts.

You leave with A Lundy check of one current piece of participation work.

2Doing it well

Story. Kofi, 16, is invited to a 2pm meeting at County Hall to give his views on the leaving care offer. He would miss college to get there, nobody has said what the meeting will decide, and there is no mention of expenses.

Theory. Space and voice in Lundy’s model are practical: a safe place young people can get to, information in advance, support to say what they think, and fair payment for their time. Harry Shier’s pathways to participation (2001) adds a check at each level: is the service ready, is there a real opportunity, and is it written into how the service works?

Action. Groups redesign one real session with a planning sheet: the decision it feeds, preparation, consent, safeguarding, timing, place, expenses and payment, until it meets all four parts of the Lundy model.

By the end of this part people can plan a session young people can get to and take part in safely, with preparation, consent and payment agreed in advance.

You leave with A redesigned session plan that meets all four parts of the Lundy model.

3Making it count

Story. Nia, 14, tells her independent reviewing officer, a survey and a participation worker the same thing in the same month: she wants to see her younger brother more. Three adults wrote it down. None of them could say who would decide.

Theory. This is audience and influence: views need to reach a named person with the power to act, by an agreed route and time. The corporate parenting principles require councils to take children’s views, wishes and feelings into account, and Ofsted’s care leavers judgement looks at young people’s ability to influence the services they receive.

Action. Participation staff and managers map together how views travel in your service, from a session to the person who decides, including the corporate parenting board. They mark where views get stuck and agree who owns each step.

By the end of this part people can map the route from a participation session to a named decision-maker, and name where views currently stop.

You leave with A mapped route from a session to a named decision-maker in your service.

4Closing the loop

Story. Harley, 13, asked for later bedtimes at weekends in his children’s home. The answer was no. The manager who decided told him why within a fortnight. He came to the next meeting anyway.

Theory. Feedback is part of influence. Young people need to hear what was decided and why, including when the answer is no. An honest no, given quickly by the person who decided, keeps trust. Silence loses it. You said, we did, and why not is how a service shows that to young people and to a board or inspector.

Action. Groups write a you said, we did, and why not response to a real set of views, check it for plain language, and build a closing-the-loop plan with a named owner and a date for each piece of work.

By the end of this part people can write feedback young people understand, including an honest no, and plan when and how it will reach them.

You leave with A closing-the-loop plan with named owners and dates.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the day structured?

Four parts, each built the same way. Story: a young person’s experience of having their say, such as Tegan’s welcome pack that went nowhere or Harley’s honest no. Theory: the model that explains it, from Hart’s ladder of participation to Lundy’s space, voice, audience and influence. Action: a tool your team practises on its own live work: the Lundy check, a redesigned session plan, a mapped decision route and a closing-the-loop plan. Face to face it is a 6-hour day, 10am to 4pm, with time to work on your own routes and live projects. Live online it is one 2-hour session covering the key story, theory and tool from each part, with the workbook and on-demand course to go deeper.

Why do managers need to come, not only participation staff?

Because the gap is usually between the session and the decision. Managers hear what young people need from them, and both groups leave with the same plan for getting views to decisions and answers back.

Does it cover children in care councils?

Yes. Children in care council leads are part of the audience. The course looks at how council members’ views reach corporate parents and services, and how members hear what happened next.

Which framework does it use?

The Lundy model of participation, developed by Professor Laura Lundy in 2007 to put Article 12 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child into practice, alongside Roger Hart’s ladder of participation. We link both to the corporate parenting principles on seeking and taking account of children’s views. If your service already has a participation strategy or standard, we work within it.

What will we have to show for it afterwards?

A mapped route from participation to decisions in your service, and a closing-the-loop plan with named owners and dates. Both give your corporate parenting board something concrete to review.

How do you price it?

We quote per cohort, based on how many people, the format and how much tailoring you want. Tell us what you need and we will send you a clear quote.
