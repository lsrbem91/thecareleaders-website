Voice

Someone who has been there.

A three-day programme for care-experienced adults who want to mentor a child in care, and for the council or virtual school setting up the scheme. The days run a month apart, so mentors can try things out and bring questions back. They learn to build trust, share their own story safely, set goals with a child using the GROW model and pass on a worry straight away, so children get an adult alongside them who has been there.

Length
Three one-day sessions, a month apart
How
Face to face
Group
One cohort of new mentors, with the staff who will support them
For
Care-experienced adults becoming mentors

The problem it solves

Councils and virtual schools want care-experienced adults to mentor children in care, because someone who has been there can reach a child in ways professionals cannot. New mentors are often unsure how much of their own story to share, where friendship ends and the role begins, and what to do when a child tells them something worrying. Mentoring can also bring a mentor’s own memories of care back to the surface. Without training and support, good mentors drop out and the child loses another adult. Each part of this programme starts with a moment from a child’s side, gives mentors the research that explains it, and ends with something they practise in pairs and take into their mentoring.

Helps withChildren’s voice and participationCare leavers, work and the local offer

What changes

  • Plan and run a first meeting that starts to build trust.
  • Decide what to share from their own story by asking whether it helps the child, and hold back when it does not.
  • Use the GROW model to help a child set a goal and plan the first step.
  • Hold clear boundaries on messaging, social media, gifts and where they meet.
  • Recognise a safeguarding concern and pass it on straight away through the scheme’s agreed route.
  • Use coaching to talk through what the role stirs up for them.
  • Have an adult alongside them who has also been in care and is there by choice, not because it is their job.
  • Set goals that are their own, and have someone notice when they reach them.
  • Are kept safe by a mentor who knows the boundaries and what to do with a worry.
  • Have an ending that is planned and talked about, not sudden.

Story

  • Describe what meeting a new adult can feel like for a child who has had many professionals come and go, and what that means for a first meeting.
  • Describe what a child may be testing when they cancel, go quiet or push a mentor away, and why endings can be hard for children in care.

Theory

  • Explain, using Jean Rhodes’s research on youth mentoring, why a close and lasting relationship is what makes mentoring work, and why relationships that end early can leave a young person worse off (Grossman and Rhodes, 2002).
  • Explain how a mentor differs from a social worker, a foster carer and an independent visitor, and describe a mentor’s part in keeping children safe under Working Together to Safeguard Children 2023.

Action

  • Use a three-question check before sharing part of their own story (who is this for, will it help the child, and am I ready to share it?), and use the GROW model (Goal, Reality, Options, Way forward) to set and review a goal with a child.
  • Respond to a worrying disclosure without promising secrecy, pass the concern on straight away through the scheme’s safeguarding route, and plan an ending with a child that names what they have achieved.
  • Care-experienced adults becoming mentors to children in care
  • Councils and virtual schools setting up or relaunching a mentoring scheme
  • Staff who will recruit, coach and support mentors
  • One cohort trained together. No qualifications or previous training are needed. Mentors should be ready to talk about their own experience of care in a safe group

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Every part opens with a moment from a child’s side, gives mentors the research or guidance that explains it and ends with something they practise in pairs. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1Foundations

Story. Day one opens with the facilitator’s own story of the adults who made a difference in care, told alongside his professional experience of building services for children. Then we meet Zainab, 12, who has had four social workers in two years. Ten minutes into meeting her new mentor she asks, “So how long are you staying?”

Theory. Jean Rhodes’s research on youth mentoring (Stand by Me, 2002) shows that mentoring helps when the relationship is close, consistent and lasts, not because of the activities. We set out how a mentor differs from a social worker, a foster carer and an independent visitor, and what a mentor does not do.

Action. A first-meeting plan. Mentors plan how they will introduce themselves to Zainab, what they will say about the role and how often they will meet, and how they will explain, from the first meeting, that they cannot keep a worry about her safety to themselves. They practise the opening in pairs.

By the end of this part mentors can explain their role in plain words to a child and a carer, and plan a first meeting that starts to build trust.

You leave with A first-meeting plan, including how you will introduce yourself.

2Theory into practice

Story. Tomasz, 15, asks his mentor if she was ever in care and what it was like. Later he says he wants to be a mechanic, but he has missed most of Year 10. A month in he cancels twice, then turns up as if nothing happened.

Theory. Sharing your own story can build trust, but only when it is for the child. John Bowlby’s attachment theory (1969) helps explain why a child who has lost adults before may test a new one by pulling away. The GROW model, set out by John Whitmore in Coaching for Performance (1992), gives mentors a simple shape for a goal conversation that leaves the child in charge of their own goal.

Action. The three-question check and GROW. Mentors decide what they would share with Tomasz using three questions: who is this for, will it help him, and am I ready to share it? Then, in pairs, they take his mechanic goal through Goal, Reality, Options and Way forward, and plan what to say when he comes back after cancelling.

By the end of this part mentors can decide what to share from their own story and when to hold back, and use GROW to set and review a goal with a child.

You leave with A GROW goal-setting sheet to use and review with a child.

3Staying well

Story. At 11pm on a Friday, Nadia, 14, messages her mentor on Instagram: “Can I tell you something, but you have to promise not to tell anyone?” Her mentor, who once sent a message like that herself, does not know what to say.

Theory. Working Together to Safeguard Children 2023 is clear that everyone who works with children shares a responsibility to keep them safe. A mentor’s job is to listen, not promise secrecy, and pass the worry on straight away, not to investigate. Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance (1999) helps mentors notice when a child’s story is taking them back to their own time in care.

Action. Rehearsing a disclosure and setting boundaries. Mentors practise answering Nadia: listening without leading questions, telling her who they will need to share it with, writing down what she said in her own words and passing it on through the scheme’s route. They then agree boundaries on messaging, social media, gifts and meeting places, and write a staying-well plan for themselves.

By the end of this part mentors can respond to a disclosure without promising secrecy, pass on a concern through the scheme’s route straight away, and say who they will talk to when the role stirs up their own memories.

You leave with Your scheme’s route for passing on a concern, and a personal plan for staying well.

4Endings

Story. After a year, Zainab’s mentor is moving away for a new job. When she tells her, Zainab says “Fine, everyone goes” and stops replying to messages.

Theory. Grossman and Rhodes (2002) found that mentoring relationships which ended early were linked to young people doing worse than before. For children who have had adults leave suddenly, how a relationship ends can matter as much as how it started. That is why mentors plan the ending from the start.

Action. An ending plan. Mentors plan the last few meetings with Zainab: how they tell her, what they mark that she has achieved and who she can turn to next. They practise the ending conversation in pairs, and write an ending plan they can agree with a child at the start.

By the end of this part mentors can plan an ending that is talked about, names what the child has achieved and hands over to the next source of support.

You leave with A plan for ending the mentoring relationship well, agreed at the start.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the programme structured?

Three full days, a month apart, face to face. Day one covers the foundations of the role and the first meeting. Day two puts the theory into practice with sharing your story and GROW goal setting. Day three covers safeguarding, boundaries, staying well and endings. Each part follows Story, Theory, Action. Story: a moment from a child’s side, such as Zainab asking how long her mentor is staying. Theory: the research or guidance that explains it, from youth mentoring research to Working Together to Safeguard Children 2023. Action: something mentors practise in pairs, such as a first-meeting plan or responding to a disclosure.

Do mentors need to have been in care?

The programme is written for care-experienced adults, because shared experience is what the mentoring relationship is built on. Staff who will support and coach mentors can join too, so they know what mentors have been taught.

What checks do mentors need?

Your scheme needs to carry out safer recruitment checks before mentors meet children, normally including an enhanced DBS check. The council or virtual school running the scheme arranges these. The programme prepares mentors to work within your safeguarding procedures.

What if the training brings up difficult memories?

The programme is built with this in mind. Nobody has to share more than they choose, day three includes a plan for staying well, and we agree with you who mentors can talk to between sessions.

How do you price it?

We quote per cohort, based on how many people, the format and how much tailoring you want. Tell us what you need and we will send you a clear quote.

Who delivers it?

People with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services. Every programme is designed by Luke Rodgers BEM and follows the same Story, Theory, Action approach.
