Voice

Someone who has been there.

Training for care-experienced adults who want to mentor children in care. It covers what makes a good mentor, how to share your story safely, and how to keep yourself and the child safe, so the relationship can do what professionals cannot.

Length
Three days, a month apart
How
Face to face
Group
A cohort of mentors
For
Care-experienced people
Ask about this course
The problem it solves

Councils and virtual schools want care-experienced adults to mentor children in care, because someone who has been there can reach a child in ways professionals cannot. Without proper training, mentors are unsure what to share, where the boundaries sit and when to pass a worry on.

Helps withChildren’s voice and participationCare leavers, work and the local offer

What changes

  • Plan and run a first meeting that starts to build trust.
  • Share their own story when it helps the child, and hold back when not.
  • Listen, encourage and set goals with a child.
  • Know when and how to pass on a safeguarding concern.
  • Have an adult alongside them who has also been in care.
  • Set their own goals with someone who listens.
  • Are kept safe by a mentor who knows the boundaries.
  • Have an ending that is planned, not sudden.
  • What a mentor is, and what they are not
  • Sharing your story in a way that helps, not hurts
  • Listening, encouraging and setting goals together
  • Boundaries, safeguarding and when to pass something on
  • Looking after yourself along the way
  • Care-experienced adults becoming mentors

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Every part opens with a real story, adds the evidence behind it and ends with something people use the next day. Open a part to see what happens.

1Foundations

What a mentor is and is not, befriending, building trust, and planning the first meeting with a child.

You leave with A plan for the first meeting with a child.

2Theory into practice

What helps children grow, and how a mentor fits in: listening, encouraging and setting goals together.

You leave with A simple way to set and review goals with a child.

3Staying well

Boundaries, safeguarding and support, including when to pass something on and how mentors look after themselves.

You leave with A clear route for passing on concerns, and a plan for self-care.

4Endings

Moving on well, for both of you: planning an ending so the child keeps what the relationship gave them.

You leave with A plan for ending the mentoring relationship well.

Questions commissioners ask

Do mentors need to have been in care?

The course is written for care-experienced adults, because shared experience is what the mentoring relationship is built on. Staff who will support and supervise mentors can join too, so they know what mentors have been taught.

Can our own staff train future mentors?

Yes. There is a train the trainer option, so your staff can run the course with new cohorts of mentors using the How to be a Mentor workbook.

Can you shape it around our service?

Yes. We tailor the examples, the length and the mix of people in the room to your service, your procedures and the children you work with.

How do you price it?

We quote per cohort, based on how many people, the format and how much tailoring you want. Tell us what you need and we will send you a clear quote.

Who delivers it?

People with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services. Every course is designed by Luke Rodgers BEM and follows the same Story, Theory, Action approach.
