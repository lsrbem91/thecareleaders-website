Voice

Co-production, not tokenism.

For leaders, commissioners, participation staff and care-experienced adults who work in or with children’s services. The course shows how to involve people with lived experience of care so it is safe, paid and changes decisions, taught by people who have been on both sides: care experienced, and professionals who have led change. Care-experienced people get roles that last, and children get services shaped by people who understand them.

Length
6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm. 2 hours live online
How
Face to face or live online
Group
Up to 60 people
For
Leaders, commissioners, councillors and participation teams

The problem it solves

Services want to involve care-experienced people, and young people are asking to be heard directly rather than talked about. Too often it means one person telling their story at one event, unpaid, with no preparation and no support afterwards, and nothing in the decision changes. That can harm the person involved and leaves the service with little real influence to show for it. A story told once and clapped is not co-production. Each part of this course starts with what involvement is like from the contributor’s side, gives you the theory that explains what went wrong, and ends with a safeguard, a written position or a role your service can put in place.

What changes

  • Plan involvement with informed consent, wellbeing support and clear boundaries from the start
  • Pay and credit care-experienced contributors properly, and check how payment affects their benefits
  • Give care-experienced people a part in decisions, and tell them what changed as a result
  • Prepare people to share only what they choose, and debrief with them afterwards
  • Build roles that carry on after a single project ends
  • Services shaped by people who understand what being in care is like
  • Care-experienced contributors who share only what they choose, with support before and after
  • Contributors who are paid and credited for their time and expertise
  • Hearing back about what their contribution changed
  • Routes into longer roles and leadership

Story

  • Describe what being involved is like from a care-experienced contributor’s side, before, during and after they share their experience.
  • Recognise involvement in your own service that is one-off, unpaid or unsupported, and what it costs the person involved.

Theory

  • Explain, using Miranda Fricker’s idea of testimonial injustice (2007), what lived experience adds alongside research and practice evidence, and what it should never be asked to carry.
  • Use Sherry Arnstein’s ladder of citizen participation (1969), Roger Hart’s ladder of participation (1992) and Laura Lundy’s model (2007) to test whether involvement in your service gives people real influence.

Action

  • Plan involvement that avoids re-traumatisation, using SAMHSA’s trauma-informed principles (2014): informed consent, choice over what is shared, preparation and debrief.
  • Set a fair, written position on payment and recognition, and design a role for care-experienced people that lasts beyond one project, with training, supervision and a route into leadership.
  • Directors, heads of service and service managers
  • Commissioners, policy and strategy teams
  • Participation, engagement and co-production staff
  • Elected members and corporate parenting boards
  • Care-experienced adults who work in or with services, who take part alongside staff
  • Up to 60 people. No prior knowledge is needed.

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Every part starts with involvement seen from the contributor’s side, gives you the theory that explains it, and ends with something your service can put in place the next day. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1Why it matters

Story. Danielle, 22, left care at 18 and now works as a support worker. At a meeting about the leaving care offer she points out that the new training flat is a 40-minute bus ride from the college most care leavers go to. Nobody else in the room had ever taken that bus.

Theory. Miranda Fricker’s idea of testimonial injustice (2007) describes what happens when someone’s knowledge is given less weight because of who they are. Lived experience is evidence of what a service is like to use. It sits beside research and practice evidence, it doesn’t replace them, and it should never be asked to carry a decision on its own.

Action. Groups take a decision their service is making now and set out what research, practice and lived experience each tell them about it, then name what is missing and who could fill the gap. Each group shares its gap, and the room agrees where lived experience would change the decision most.

By the end of this part people can explain what lived experience adds to a decision alongside research and practice evidence, and what it should not be asked to carry.

You leave with A shared view of why involvement matters, and its limits.

2Doing it safely

Story. Jamal, 19, is asked to tell his story at a conference. He gets a slot, a microphone and no idea who is in the audience. Afterwards everyone claps and goes to lunch. He goes home on his own and doesn’t sleep.

Theory. SAMHSA’s six principles for a trauma-informed approach (2014) include safety, trustworthiness and transparency, peer support and choice. For someone sharing their own experience, that means knowing exactly what they are agreeing to, choosing what to share and what to keep back, and having someone with them before and after.

Action. Window of Tolerance and a safeguards plan. Groups replan Jamal’s slot from the first phone call: what he is told, what he chooses to share, who prepares with him, what overwhelm looks like for him and how he can pause, and who checks in afterwards. Each group then writes the safeguards your service will agree before anyone shares.

By the end of this part people can plan involvement with informed consent, choice over what is shared, preparation and a debrief, and agree how someone can pause if it becomes too much.

You leave with Safeguards to agree before anyone is asked to share.

3Doing it fairly

Story. Owen, 20, sits on a lived experience panel. He is thanked in every report and paid in vouchers, months late. The panel’s advice on the new housing policy was “noted”.

Theory. Sherry Arnstein’s ladder of citizen participation (1969) and Roger Hart’s ladder of participation (1992) both show the gap between being consulted and having a say. Laura Lundy’s model (2007) tests whether there is a real audience and real influence. However often Owen’s panel meets, it sits near the bottom of both ladders.

Action. Groups place their own involvement on the ladder, then draft a written position on pay and recognition: what you pay for, expenses, how quickly people are paid, and checking how payment could affect someone’s benefits before they agree to take part. Groups then swap drafts and test each other’s against the questions a care-experienced contributor would ask.

By the end of this part people can place their current practice on a ladder of participation and draft a fair, written position on payment and recognition.

You leave with A clear position on pay and recognition for your service.

4Making it last

Story. Courtney, 24, joined a care leavers’ forum at 16, became a paid peer mentor at 19 and now co-chairs a corporate parenting sub-group. Each step happened because a manager made a real role for her.

Theory. From care leaver to care leader. Involvement that lasts needs what any career needs: a paid role, training, supervision and somewhere to go next. Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun’s work on post-traumatic growth (1996) describes how people can find new strength and purpose through hard experiences. It is never a reason to ask someone to relive them.

Action. Post-Traumatic Growth and the involvement checklist. Groups check current practice against the checklist, sketch one role that lasts beyond a single project, and agree the first change to make, with a name and a date against it. Each group shares its first change so the room leaves with one list.

By the end of this part people can design a role for care-experienced people that lasts beyond one project, and name the first change their service will make.

You leave with A lived experience involvement checklist for your service.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the day structured?

Four parts, each built the same way. Story: involvement seen from the contributor’s side, such as Jamal going home alone after a conference or Owen’s advice being “noted”. Theory: the idea that explains it, from testimonial injustice to Arnstein’s and Hart’s ladders and the Lundy model. Action: something your service puts in place: safeguards to agree before anyone shares, a written position on pay and recognition, and an involvement checklist. Face to face it is a 6-hour day, 10am to 4pm, covering all four parts. Live online it is one 2-hour session covering the key story, theory and tool from each part, with the workbook and on-demand course to go deeper. Care-experienced adults who work in or with your service take part alongside staff.

We already have a lived experience panel. Will this help?

Yes. We use the Lundy model to look at whether your panel has real influence, how members are paid and supported, and how roles can grow into leadership rather than stopping at consultation.

Will you tell us what to pay people?

We don’t set a rate for you. We help you agree a clear, written position: what you pay for, expenses, how quickly people are paid, and checking how payment could affect someone’s benefits before they agree to take part.

Does it fit with our corporate parenting duties?

Yes. The corporate parenting principles in the Children and Social Work Act 2017 include taking into account the views, wishes and feelings of children in care and care leavers. The course shows how to do that in a way that changes decisions, and suits corporate parenting boards.

Who delivers it?

Trainers with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services. The course was designed by Luke Rodgers BEM, who grew up in care and has spent his career leading change in children’s services, and it follows our Story, Theory, Action approach.

How do you price it?

We quote per cohort, based on how many people, the format and how much tailoring you want. Tell us what you need and we will send you a clear quote.
