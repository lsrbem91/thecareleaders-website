Voice

Teach it the way we do.

A facilitator programme for in-house trainers, participation workers and care-experienced facilitators who want to design and run their own sessions about children’s lives. Over two days, or four live online sessions, you learn the method behind every Care Leaders course: open with a real story, teach the theory that explains it, and end with a tool people use the next day. By the end you have planned and run a session of your own and had feedback on it.

The problem it solves

Services want in-house trainers and care-experienced facilitators to run their own sessions, but people are often handed someone else’s slides rather than a method. Sessions then lean on theory with no child in it, or on a personal story with no learning attached, which can leave the storyteller exposed and the room with nothing to do differently. This programme practises what it teaches. Every part opens with a story, gives you the adult learning theory behind it and ends with a piece of your own session, built and tested in the room.

Helps withBuilding in-house champions and trainers

What changes

  • Choose a story for a clear learning point, and decide what to leave out
  • Link each story to the theory or guidance that explains it, and teach it in a few minutes without jargon
  • End every session with an activity or tool people can use at work the next day
  • Plan timings, handle questions and difficult moments, and look after themselves and the group
  • Share their own lived experience safely, if they choose to, alongside the professional experience they bring
  • Staff trained through real examples of children’s lives, not only slides
  • Experiences of care shared with consent and a clear purpose
  • Adults who come back from training with something practical to try with them

Story

  • Select and shape a story for one clear learning point, checking consent, privacy and what to leave out.
  • Decide when to share their own lived experience in training and when not to, and prepare an answer for questions they do not want to answer.

Theory

  • Explain the Story, Theory, Action structure using David Kolb’s experiential learning cycle (1984) and Malcolm Knowles’s principles of adult learning (1970), and say why a story, a theory and a tool together help adults change what they do.
  • Match a story to a theory or piece of guidance, check it against the original source, and teach it in a few minutes without overloading the room.

Action

  • Design an activity or tool that turns a session’s learning into a change at work, and plan the whole session with timings.
  • Run the session to a group, respond to difficult moments in the room, including distress and disclosures, and use feedback to improve it.
  • In-house trainers and workforce development teams in local authorities and providers
  • Care-experienced adults who facilitate training, or want to
  • Participation and youth engagement workers
  • Designated teachers, practice leads and senior practitioners who train colleagues
  • Up to 12 people. No training experience is needed, but people should expect to train others afterwards

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

The programme uses its own method. Every part opens with a story from a facilitator’s side, gives you the theory that explains it and ends with a piece of your own session that you build and test with the group. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1Story

Story. A facilitator with lived experience of care tells a story, then the group takes it apart: why that story, why told that way, and what was left out. Then we meet Ife, 23, care experienced, who was asked to “share her story” at a foster carer training evening. She told all of it. A carer asked about her mum in front of 40 people, and Ife went home and did not sleep.

Theory. Lived experience in training is there for a purpose, and the person telling it stays in charge of it. We use SAMHSA’s principles for a trauma-informed approach (2014), especially safety, choice and peer support, to show what a safe invitation to share looks like, and why consent to tell a story can be taken back at any time.

Action. Shaping a story. Each person picks a story for a session they will run and shapes it around one learning point: what it is for, what to leave out, whose consent they need and what stays private. Care-experienced facilitators write and practise an answer for a question they do not want to answer, so they keep control of their own story.

By the end of this part people can choose and shape a story for one learning point, and say what they will and will not share and how they will answer a question they do not want to.

You leave with A story shaped for a session you will run, checked for consent and safety

2Theory

Story. Gareth, a senior practitioner, opens a session on attachment with 20 slides on Bowlby and Ainsworth. By slide 9 the room is checking phones. A week later nobody can say what attachment has to do with the children they work with.

Theory. John Sweller’s cognitive load theory (1988) explains why people can only take in a little new information at once, so a theory lands when it is short, tied to one story and taught as one or two ideas. We cover how to pair a story with evidence such as attachment, trauma or child development, or guidance such as Working Together to Safeguard Children 2023, and how to check a source before you use it.

Action. The short theory. In pairs, people match a theory to their story, read the original source, and teach it to their partner in a few minutes in plain words. Their partner checks it for accuracy and jargon, and they cut it down again.

By the end of this part people can match a theory or piece of guidance to their story, check it against the source and teach it briefly and accurately.

You leave with A theory matched to your story, with a short explanation you can teach

3Action

Story. Bea works in a children’s home. After a training day on recording she says it was “really good”. On Monday she writes her logs exactly as she did before, because nothing in the day asked her to try anything.

Theory. David Kolb’s experiential learning cycle (1984) shows that adults learn by doing something, reflecting on it, making sense of it and trying it again, so people need to practise, not only hear about it. Malcolm Knowles’s principles of adult learning (1970) explain why adults engage when the learning solves a problem in their work now.

Action. Designing the activity. People design the activity or tool that closes their session, such as a script, a checklist, reflective questions or a role play, then test it on the group and change it based on what happened.

By the end of this part people can design an activity that has learners practise something they will use at work the next day, and plan their whole session with timings.

You leave with An activity or tool to close your session, tested with the group

4Your session

Story. Ife runs her first session to the group. Ten minutes in, someone in the room goes quiet and leaves; the story has touched something of their own.

Theory. Donald Schön’s idea of the reflective practitioner (1983) separates reflection-in-action, adjusting while you run the session, from reflection-on-action, looking back afterwards. We use both: how to respond in the moment to distress or a disclosure, and how to use feedback afterwards to improve the session.

Action. Running your session. Each person runs a short session they have designed, and gets feedback from the group and the facilitator on content, timing, energy and how they handled questions. We also rehearse what to do if someone becomes upset or discloses something: pause, check in privately and follow the service’s safeguarding procedures.

By the end of this part people can run a session they designed, respond safely to a difficult moment in the room, and use feedback to improve the session plan.

You leave with A session plan you have run once, with written feedback to improve it

Questions commissioners ask

How is the programme structured?

Four parts, run as two days or four live online sessions. Each part follows Story, Theory, Action, and each one builds a piece of your own session. Story: shaping a story safely, using composite examples such as Ife being asked to share her story. Theory: the adult learning theory behind the method, such as Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, and how to teach a theory briefly. Action: designing the activity that closes your session. In part 4 everyone runs their session and gets feedback. Most people bring a session they need to run, and use the programme to build it.

Do people need training experience already?

No, but they should expect to train colleagues or young people afterwards. Groups are kept to 12 so everyone designs and runs a session, whether they are new to it or experienced.

Is it only for care-experienced facilitators?

No. It suits in-house trainers, participation workers, designated teachers and practice leads. Care-experienced facilitators get specific support on sharing their lived experience safely, alongside the professional experience they bring.

How do you keep care-experienced facilitators safe?

The Story part covers consent, choosing what to share and what to keep private, and preparing an answer for questions people do not want to answer. We also ask services to have supervision or a named person in place for facilitators after they run sessions.

Does it license people to deliver your courses?

No. It teaches the method so people can build and run their own training. It is not a licence to deliver The Care Leaders’ courses.

How do you price it?

We quote per cohort, based on how many people, the format and how much tailoring you want. Tell us what you need and we will send you a clear quote.
