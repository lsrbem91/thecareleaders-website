Power to Lead: leadership training for young people in careYoung people leading the change.

A leadership course for young people aged 13 to 25 who are in care or have left care, run for children in care councils, youth ambassador groups and care leaver forums. In one day, young people decide which parts of their story they share and which stay private, practise making their point in a room full of adults, and plan a small project they will lead. It is the place to start before The Care Leaders Academy.

Length
6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm. 2 hours live online
How
Face to face or live online
Group
Up to 60 people
For
Young people aged 13 to 25 in care or leaving care

The problem it solves

Children in care councils and youth ambassador groups are asked to speak in rooms full of senior adults, often with little preparation. The same few confident voices carry the load, and young people can share more of their story than they meant to, then feel exposed afterwards. Councils have a duty to encourage children in care and care leavers to express their views. Doing that safely takes preparation. Power to Lead gives it: every part starts with a story young people will recognise, explains how power and decisions really work, and ends with something they practise there and then.

Helps withChildren’s voice and participation

What changes

  • Prepare young people before they meet senior adults: who will be in the room, what is being decided and what their views can change.
  • Respect a young person’s choice to keep parts of their story private, and never ask them to retell it for effect.
  • Share roles around the group, so it is not the same one or two young people who speak every time.
  • Back each young person’s project with a named adult, and review their leadership plan with them after the course.
  • Know their strengths and can talk about them with an example.
  • Choose what to share about their story, and have the words ready to turn down a question.
  • Know who makes decisions in their council and how to get in front of them.
  • Lead a small project of their own, with a plan and an adult who backs it.

Story

  • Describe three of their own strengths, with an example of each from their own life.
  • Decide which parts of their story they will share, with whom and when, and turn down a question they do not want to answer.

Theory

  • Explain Laura Lundy’s model of participation (2007), space, voice, audience and influence, and use it to judge whether a meeting they attend can change anything.
  • Explain who makes decisions in their council, including corporate parents, elected members and senior managers, and what the corporate parenting principles say about hearing young people’s views.

Action

  • Make a two-minute point to a group of adults using a structure they have practised, and answer one challenging question.
  • Write a plan for a small project with a SMART goal, the first three steps and the adult who will back it.
  • Children in care councils and care leaver forums.
  • Youth ambassador, shadow board and participation groups.
  • Young people aged 13 to 25. We shape the examples and activities to the ages in the group, and can run separate groups for 13 to 17 and 18 to 25.
  • Up to 60 people. A worker they know is welcome to join.
  • No experience of leadership or public speaking needed.

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Every part opens with a story young people will recognise, explains the idea behind it and ends with something they practise in the room. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1Me

Story. The part opens with a story of growing up in care and going on to lead change. Then Keisha, 15, who was asked to speak at a corporate parenting board. She told them about her worst placement move because she thought that was what they wanted to hear. The room clapped. She went home and could not sleep.

Theory. The LUUT model, from Barnett Pearce and Vernon Cronen’s Coordinated Management of Meaning, separates the story you lived from the story you tell. Your lived story belongs to you. The story you tell is a choice, and it can change depending on who is listening and why.

Action. Young people list their strengths and find an example of each. Then they sort their own story into three piles: what I share with anyone, what I share with people I trust, and what stays mine. They practise a line for turning down a question, such as “That’s not something I talk about here, but what I can tell you is...”

By the end of this part people can name three strengths with an example of each, say what they will share and what stays private, and turn down a question calmly.

You leave with A list of their strengths and a personal plan for what they share and what stays private.

2Us

Story. Connor, 16, is the one who always speaks at the children in care council. When he is off sick, the meeting with the director goes quiet. Nobody else has ever been asked to speak, so nobody does.

Theory. Bruce Tuckman’s stages of group development (1965), forming, storming, norming and performing, show why a new group feels awkward at first and why it gets stronger once everyone has a job. A group carried by one voice stops being a group.

Action. The group agrees roles: chair, speaker, note-taker, timekeeper and the person who asks the follow-up question. Each young person builds a short point in three lines: what is happening, why it matters, what we want to change. They say it to the group, then to facilitators playing a tougher room, and use Rewind and Replay to go back over the moment that wobbled and try it again.

By the end of this part people can make a two-minute point to a group of adults, answer a challenging question, and name the role they will take in their group.

You leave with A practised way to make a point in meetings with adults, and a role in their group.

3Them

Story. Imani, 19, and her care leaver forum spent a year asking for help with travel costs. They met managers, filled in feedback forms and were thanked every time. Nothing changed. Then they found out the decision sat with someone nobody had invited them to meet.

Theory. Laura Lundy’s model of participation (2007) says young people need four things: space to speak, support to give their voice, an audience who can act, and influence over what happens next. Most groups get the first two. Roger Hart’s ladder of participation (1992) helps young people tell the difference between being consulted and being there for show. We also explain the corporate parenting principles in the Children and Social Work Act 2017, which require the council to encourage children in care and care leavers to express their views and to take them into account.

Action. Young people pick an issue they care about and draw a power map: who decides, who influences that person, when the decision gets made and how to get in front of them. They check their plan against Lundy’s four steps and fix the gaps.

By the end of this part people can explain the difference between corporate parents, elected members and managers, and name one decision-maker for their issue and how to reach them.

You leave with A map of who decides on their issue and how to reach them.

4Next

Story. Finn, 14, thinks the welcome pack for children coming into care should be written by young people. Everyone says it is a great idea. Six months later nothing has happened, and the worker who said it has moved on.

Theory. George Doran’s SMART goals (1981): specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound. A good idea becomes a project when it has a clear goal, a first step and a date.

Action. Each young person or group writes a leadership plan: their SMART goal, the first three steps, the adult who will back it and the date they will check progress. They present it to the staff from their service, who agree there and then what they will do to help.

By the end of this part people can write a SMART goal for their project, set out the first three steps, and name the adult who will support them.

You leave with A personal leadership plan for each young person.

Choose how you run it

We come to a space young people know, or a venue you choose, for one day in the holidays or at a weekend, 10am to 4pm. The day covers all four parts, with time to practise in the room and start the project plans.

Length
One day, 10am to 4pm
Group
Up to 60 people
You provide
A room set up for small-group work with space to move around, a screen with HDMI, travel and food for young people, and a named person from your service to pick up the leadership plans afterwards. A worker young people know is welcome to stay for the day.

A 2-hour live session on Teams or Zoom, covering the core of the course: the key story, theory and tool from each part, with the workbook and on-demand course to go deeper. Breakout rooms are used for the practice, and each young person leaves with a first draft of their leadership plan.

One 2-hour live session on Teams or Zoom

Included for every delegate: a workbook to keep, the recording of the session for 12 months, an interactive on-demand course on The Care Leaders Online for 12 months, and a certificate of completion.

Over 60 people, we bring a second facilitator so everyone still gets the time the activities need.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the day structured?

Four parts: Me, Us, Them and Next. Each one starts with a story young people will recognise, such as Keisha sharing more than she meant to at a board meeting. Then the idea that explains it, from the LUUT model to Laura Lundy’s model of participation. Then something they practise in the room, from a line for turning down a question to a power map and a project plan. Face to face it runs as one day, usually 10am to 4pm. Online it is one 2-hour live session covering the core of each part. It began as five sessions with Youth Ambassadors from across the South East, January to May 2025.

How does this fit with The Care Leaders Academy?

Power to Lead is the place to start. The Care Leaders Academy is the full programme for young people aged 13 to 18, over several weeks with live tasks. Power to Lead can stand alone or lead into it.

What ages is it for?

Young people aged 13 to 25 who are in care or have left care. We shape the examples and activities around the ages in the group, and plan the activities so everyone takes part. If your group has a wide age range, we can run separate sessions for younger and older members.

Will young people be asked to share their story?

No. Choosing what to share, and what to keep private, is the first thing the course covers. Nobody is asked to retell their story, and every story we use as an example is a composite.

Do our staff need to be there?

A worker young people know is welcome to stay for the day. What matters most is what happens afterwards: each young person leaves with a leadership plan, and someone from your service needs to pick it up with them so the project goes ahead.

Who delivers it?

People with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services. Every course is designed by Luke Rodgers BEM and follows the same Story, Theory, Action approach.
