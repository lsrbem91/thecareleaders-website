Be the First Yes: training for employers hiring care leaversBe the employer who says yes first.

For employers, HR and early careers teams who want to offer work experience, apprenticeships and first jobs to care-experienced young people aged 16 to 25. In one day you find the points in your own recruitment where care leavers drop out, fix them, and leave with a written pledge, so more young people get a first yes and stay in the job.

Length
6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm. 2 hours live online
How
Face to face or live online
Group
Up to 60 people
For
Employers, HR and early careers teams, and employer networks

The problem it solves

Most recruitment quietly assumes a family in the background: someone to check an application, lend the bus fare to an interview, give a reference, or cover the weeks before the first payday. Many care leavers have none of that. They drop out at the online form, the interview or the start date, and employers who genuinely wanted to help never see them apply. The talent is there. The process loses it. Each part of this course follows one young person through a recruitment process like yours, gives you the research that explains where they fall out, and ends with a change you can make to your own process the following week.

Helps withCare leavers, work and the local offer

What changes

  • Spot the points in their own advert, application and onboarding where a care leaver is likely to drop out.
  • Offer care-experienced applicants a named contact, a guaranteed interview and feedback.
  • Give applicants a voluntary, confidential way to say they are care experienced.
  • Plan the first month so nobody falls at the start line: travel costs, ID, uniform and the gap before first pay.
  • Give each care-experienced starter a workplace buddy and respond to early problems with support, not a warning.
  • Adverts and forms they can understand and finish without a parent to help.
  • Interviews that ask what they can do, not why there are gaps in their history.
  • A named person at work who knows how to help when things get difficult.
  • Opportunities they actually hear about, because employers list them through the council’s local offer.

Story

  • Describe a recruitment process from a care leaver’s side, from finding the advert to the first payday, and name the points where a family usually fills the gap.
  • Describe what care-experienced young people bring to a workplace, and why the usual signals employers look for, such as references and an unbroken education, can hide it.

Theory

  • Explain, using Mark Granovetter’s strength of weak ties (1973), why care leavers often lack the informal contacts that lead many people to a first job, and how an employer can stand in for them.
  • Explain, using Maslow’s hierarchy of needs (1943), why travel costs, ID and the wait for first pay decide whether a new starter stays, and the extra support around an apprenticeship for a care leaver, including the £3,000 DfE bursary for apprentices who start before 25 and the additional employer payment for care leavers aged 19 to 24.

Action

  • Rewrite a job advert and its application steps in plain English, with a named contact and a guaranteed interview offer, and plan a first month that does not assume family support.
  • Write a dated pledge for your organisation, using the recruitment, tracking and development commitments in the Care Leaver Covenant’s employer charter as a starting point.
  • Business owners, HR, recruitment and early careers teams in private, public and voluntary sector employers.
  • Apprenticeship and training providers who recruit on behalf of employers.
  • Chambers of commerce, employer networks and combined authority skills teams bringing several employers together.
  • Council economic development, leaving care and corporate parenting leads who want local employers involved.
  • No knowledge of the care system is needed. We explain the terms that matter, such as care leaver, personal adviser and local offer.

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Four parts of about 75 minutes, with breaks and lunch. Every part opens with a young person’s story, gives you the research that explains it and ends with a change people make to their own adverts and processes in the room. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1The talent

Story. Marley, 19, has kept a part-time job going through two moves and a college course, has managed her own money since she was 18 and looks after her younger brother at weekends. Her CV shows two schools in one year, no work reference and a gap. On paper she looks like a risk.

Theory. Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun’s research on post-traumatic growth (1996) shows that people who come through hard experiences often build real strengths, such as persistence and knowing what matters to them. We set out who care leavers are, the support they are entitled to up to 25, including a personal adviser, and why the signals employers screen on can filter those strengths out.

Action. Post-Traumatic Growth. Tables take Marley’s CV and write down what each gap shows she can do, then look at the screening criteria in one of their own adverts and mark any a care leaver could fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the job. Pairs then practise a short interview with Marley’s CV in front of them, asking questions that bring out what she can do rather than what is missing, and swap roles.

By the end of this part people can name strengths in a care leaver’s history that a standard CV hides, and identify which of their own screening criteria might screen those applicants out.

You leave with A one-page brief on care-experienced talent to share with your leadership team.

2The barriers

Story. Marley applies. The online form asks for two references and five years of addresses. The interview is across town at 9am, and the bus fare is money she won’t have until Friday. She doesn’t turn up, and the employer marks her down as not interested.

Theory. Mark Granovetter’s study The Strength of Weak Ties (1973) found that people often hear about jobs through acquaintances, a neighbour or a friend of a parent, rather than close friends. Young people who grew up in care often have fewer of those links, and fewer people to check a form or lend a fare. Recruitment quietly relies on that network, and nobody notices until someone without it applies.

Action. A recruitment route map. Teams walk Marley’s application along a timeline from advert to first payday and mark every point where a family usually fills the gap. Then they map their own process the same way and mark where an applicant like Marley would fall out. Tables compare their maps, agree the drop-out points that matter most in their own process and note who in the organisation owns each one.

By the end of this part people can map their own recruitment process from advert to first payday and mark the points where a care leaver is most likely to drop out, with the reason at each one.

You leave with A map of your own recruitment with the drop-out points marked.

3The changes

Story. Marley gets the job. In her second week she is late twice. Her phone has been cut off and she is sofa surfing after losing her room. Her manager is about to give her a written warning.

Theory. Maslow’s hierarchy of needs (1943): it is hard to focus on work when a safe place to sleep, food and the fare to get in are not sorted. Marley’s lateness is a housing problem before it is a conduct problem. We also cover how the care leaver apprenticeship bursary and employer payment work, and how a personal adviser can help an employer when things go wrong.

Action. Separating Reason from Behaviour and Language that Cares. Tables rewrite one of their own adverts in plain English, with a named contact and a guaranteed interview offer. They then plan Marley’s first month, covering travel, ID, uniform, the gap before first pay and a workplace buddy, and script what her manager says on the morning she is late instead of the warning. Pairs then practise that conversation, one as the manager and one as Marley, and swap roles.

By the end of this part people can rewrite an advert so a young person can finish the application without help, and plan a first month that does not assume family support, including the first conversation when a problem shows up.

You leave with A rewritten advert and a first-month checklist for new starters.

4The pledge

Story. Kyra, 21, did a week’s work experience at a firm that told her “we’d love to have you back”. Nobody called. Six months later she still wonders what she did wrong.

Theory. Peter Gollwitzer’s research on implementation intentions (1999) shows that a plan saying exactly who will do what, and when, is more likely to happen than a good intention. We link each plan to the council’s local offer for care leavers and the employer charter in the Care Leaver Covenant, so young people actually hear about the opportunity.

Action. Each organisation writes its 90-day pledge. Every commitment gets a named owner, a date and a first step, and a line on who in the council’s leaving care team will hear about it. Tables read each other’s pledges and point out anything that sounds like Kyra’s firm: kind words and no follow-up. Each organisation tightens its pledge in response and reads its first step out to the room.

By the end of this part people can write a dated pledge with named owners, and say how their opportunities will reach care leavers through the local offer.

You leave with A written pledge with named owners and dates, and the contacts to get your opportunities in front of care leavers.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the course structured?

Face to face it is a 6-hour day in four parts. Online it is one 2-hour live session covering the core of each part. Each part is built the same way. Story: one young person, Marley, moving through a recruitment process like yours, from the advert to her second week in the job. Theory: the research that explains where she nearly falls out, such as Granovetter’s work on how people hear about jobs or Maslow’s hierarchy of needs. Action: a change your team makes in the room, such as rewriting a real advert or planning a first month. The last part turns those changes into a dated pledge.

We are a small business. Is this still for us?

Yes. Most of the changes cost little or nothing: a named contact, a plainer advert, flexibility on references, and a buddy for the first weeks. The session also runs for groups of small employers through a chamber of commerce or employer network.

How does this connect with our council’s care leaver work?

Every council in England must publish a local offer for care leavers, including support with education, training and employment. We show employers how to get their opportunities into it and who to contact. Councils can also book the session to bring local employers together. We use the Care Leaver Covenant’s employer charter as a practical checklist because its commitments on recruitment, tracking and development are clear, but you do not have to sign the Covenant. That is your decision.

How will we know if an applicant is care experienced?

Only if they choose to tell you. We cover how to offer a voluntary, confidential question and why some young people will not want to answer it. For apprenticeships, a care leaver aged 19 to 24 can choose not to share their status with their employer, in which case the employer payment is not made. We explain how to handle that respectfully.

How do you price it?

We quote per session or per cohort, based on how many people, the format and how much tailoring you want. Tell us what you need and we will send you a clear quote.

Who delivers it?

People with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services, who also know how recruitment works from the employer’s side. Every course is designed by Luke Rodgers BEM and follows the same Story, Theory, Action approach.
