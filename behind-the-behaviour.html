Behind the Behaviour: training on behaviour as communicationEvery behaviour is a message.

For school staff, residential workers and foster carers who support children in care and children who need the most support. Your staff learn to read what a behaviour is telling them, stay calm through the hardest five minutes, put things right afterwards and write it up fairly. Fewer moments turn into incidents, and children keep the adults who help them settle. The schools version follows the DfE guidance on restrictive interventions in force from April 2026.

Length
6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm. 2 hours live online
How

Group

The problem it solves

A child refuses, shouts or shuts down, and the adult reads it as defiance. A warning or sanction follows, the child escalates, and a moment that could have been managed becomes an incident, an exclusion or a placement under strain. The record afterwards often calls the child “aggressive” or “manipulative” instead of saying what happened and what helped, and children in care carry those records with them. Behaviour is communication. Each part of this course starts with a moment like that, seen from the child’s side, gives your staff the theory that explains it, and ends with a tool they use the next morning.

Helps withBehaviour, incidents and restraintTrauma-informed practice

What changes

  • Pause and ask what a behaviour might be telling them before choosing a response.
  • Notice when a child has left their window of tolerance and stop reasoning with a child who is too distressed to hear it.
  • Use PACE and calm, low-demand language to help the child settle first, connect second and talk it through last.
  • Hold a short repair conversation with the child after an incident, alongside any consequence.
  • Write incident records that describe what happened, what came before and what helped, without labels.
  • Adults who stay calm and do not take it personally when things go wrong.
  • Being helped to calm down before being asked to explain.
  • A chance to put things right and start the next day fresh.
  • Records that describe what happened, not who they are.

Story

  • Describe a refusal or outburst from the child’s side: what they might have been feeling, what they needed and how the adult’s words landed.
  • Recognise the early signs that a child they support is moving out of their window of tolerance, and the common triggers in their own setting.

Theory

  • Explain, using Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance (1999) and Bowlby’s attachment theory (1969), how trauma, loss and early relationships shape a child’s stress response.
  • Explain Bruce Perry’s Regulate, Relate, Reason sequence, and describe what their setting’s rules require when restrictive intervention is considered: the DfE schools guidance in force from April 2026, or regulations 20 and 35 of the Children’s Homes (England) Regulations 2015.

Action

  • Use PACE (playfulness, acceptance, curiosity and empathy) and Spot, Soothe, Shift to de-escalate a tense moment without threats or ultimatums.
  • Plan a repair conversation with a child and write an incident record that is factual, includes the child’s view and avoids labelling language.
  • Teachers, teaching assistants, pastoral leads, SENCOs and designated teachers for children in care and previously in care.
  • Children’s home staff and managers, and supported accommodation teams.
  • Foster carers, kinship carers and supervising social workers.
  • Early help and family support workers.
  • No previous training in trauma or attachment is needed, and mixed groups from different settings work well.

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Every part opens with a story from a child’s side, gives you the theory that explains it and ends with a tool people practise in the room and use the next day. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1The message

Story. Jade, 13, is told in front of the class to put her phone away. She swears and walks out. Her teacher writes “defiant”. What Jade knows, and the teacher doesn’t, is that the last time an adult took her phone she lost contact with her mum for a week.

Theory. Behaviour is communication. We separate what a child does from the reason underneath it, and show why a warning or sanction given in the moment usually answers the behaviour and misses the reason.

Action. Separating Reason from Behaviour. In pairs, staff take a moment from their own week, write down what the child did, then list three possible reasons from the child’s side, before deciding what a response to the reason would look like.

By the end of this part people can describe a behaviour from the child’s side and name at least two possible reasons behind it before they respond.

You leave with Separating Reason from Behaviour, and five questions to ask yourself before responding to a behaviour.

2The story underneath

Story. Kai, 14, has moved three times this year. In his new home he goes silent and stays in his room whenever his carer raises her voice, even when she is laughing on the phone.

Theory. Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance (1999) explains why a child whose early world was not safe can tip into fight, flight or shutdown at things other people barely notice. Bowlby’s attachment theory (1969) explains why that shows up most around the adults closest to them. Kai’s silence is a stress response, not rudeness.

Action. Window of Tolerance. Staff map an anonymised child they support: what happened to them, what tips them out of the window, what it looks like when they go up (shouting, running) or down (silence, switching off), and what brings them back.

By the end of this part people can explain the window of tolerance in plain words to a colleague, and map one child’s triggers and early signs onto it.

You leave with A one-page Window of Tolerance map linking a child’s history to what they do now.

3In the moment

Story. Mason, 9, is under a table in the classroom, kicking at anyone who comes close. Three adults are talking to him at once, and each one is asking him a question.

Theory. Bruce Perry’s Regulate, Relate, Reason: a child has to feel calm before they can connect, and connect before they can think it through. Dan Hughes’s PACE describes how the adult stays alongside the child while that happens. For schools, we cover what the DfE’s 2026 guidance on restrictive interventions says about prevention, de-escalation and seclusion.

Action. PACE and Spot, Soothe, Shift. Staff replay Mason’s moment in threes, with one adult, fewer words and low-demand phrases, then rehearse scenarios from their own classrooms and homes and swap roles so everyone practises as the adult.

By the end of this part people can use PACE phrases and the Spot, Soothe, Shift steps to bring a tense moment down without threats or ultimatums, and say what their setting’s rules expect before any physical intervention.

You leave with De-escalation steps and phrases, written on a card staff keep with them.

4After the moment

Story. The next morning Jade walks into school expecting to be sent home. Nobody mentions yesterday. The incident record says she was “aggressive and abusive”. One day she will read it.

Theory. Relationships survive hard moments when an adult comes back and repairs them, and children in care often have little experience of that happening. We cover the school duty to record significant incidents involving force and to tell parents or carers in writing, and record-keeping in children’s homes under regulation 35 of the Children’s Homes (England) Regulations 2015.

Action. Rewind and Replay. Staff rewind one incident and replay it from the child’s side, plan the first two minutes of a repair conversation, then rewrite a sample incident record together so it describes what happened, what came before, what helped and what the child said.

By the end of this part people can plan and open a repair conversation, and write an incident record with no labels that a child could read without shame.

You leave with A repair conversation guide and a checklist for recording incidents.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the day structured?

Four parts, each built the same way. Story: a moment from the child’s side, such as Jade walking out of class or Mason under a table. Theory: the idea that explains it, from the window of tolerance to Regulate, Relate, Reason. Action: a tool staff practise in the room, such as PACE or Rewind and Replay, and use the next day. The course runs for 6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm, with breaks, which leaves time for practice and planning in every part. Live online it is a 2-hour session covering the key story, theory and tool from each part.

Does it cover the 2026 guidance on restrictive interventions?

Yes. The schools version follows the DfE’s Restrictive interventions, including use of reasonable force, in schools, in force from April 2026, including prevention, de-escalation, recording significant incidents and informing parents or carers. It is not physical intervention training. If staff need restraint techniques, they come from a specialist provider, and this course sits alongside that.

Does it work for children’s homes and foster carers?

Yes. For residential teams we use examples from homes and link the content to the positive relationships standard and the rules on restraint and behaviour records in the Children’s Homes (England) Regulations 2015. For fostering teams we use examples from family life and supervision.

Will it fit our behaviour policy?

We read your behaviour or positive relationships policy beforehand and use its language, so the course supports what you already expect staff to do. Where the course and the policy pull in different directions, we tell you.

How do you price it?

We quote per course, based on the format and how much tailoring you want. Every course is for up to 60 people, and every delegate also gets 12 months on the on-demand course, so staff who miss the day can catch up. Tell us what you need and we will send you a clear quote.

Who delivers it?

People with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services. Every course is designed by Luke Rodgers BEM and follows the same Story, Theory, Action approach.
