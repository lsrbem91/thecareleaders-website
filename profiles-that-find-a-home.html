Words that matter

A profile is a first impression.

For the social workers and placement teams who write referrals and child profiles, and the foster carers, supervising social workers and residential managers who read them. Writers learn to set out risk honestly alongside the child’s strengths, interests and own words. Readers learn to look for the child behind the incidents, so matching decisions rest on the whole child.

How

The problem it solves

A referral or profile is often all a carer or home sees before saying yes or no. Many are built from inherited case notes and incident logs, so the child is described by what went wrong. The Fostering National Minimum Standards expect carers to receive all the information the service holds before a placement, and children’s home managers weigh each referral against the needs of the children already living there. When that information is mostly risk, good matches are missed and the child arrives already labelled.

Helps withStability and matchingRecording and the words we use

What changes

  • Write referrals that set out risk in context, alongside strengths, interests and the child’s own words
  • Replace labels inherited from old case notes with plain, specific descriptions of what happened
  • Read a profile for the child behind the incidents, and ask for what is missing before deciding
  • Use one shared profile template and reading checklist across the team
  • Are introduced to carers and homes as a whole person, not a list of incidents
  • See their own views, interests and achievements in what is written about them
  • Can read what was written about them later without feeling ashamed
  • Have a better chance of a home that is ready for them
  • Explain how carers and residential managers read a referral, and what leads them to say yes or no
  • Recognise inherited labels and incident language in a referral, and rewrite them as specific, contextual descriptions
  • Write a risk section that says what happened, how often, what helps and what has changed, without defining the child by it
  • Include a child’s strengths, interests and views in their own words
  • Use a reading checklist to spot what is missing from a referral and the questions to ask before deciding
  • Describe what the Fostering National Minimum Standards and the Children’s Homes Regulations expect from information sharing and matching
  • Write a short profile, from an anonymised case of their own, that the child could read
  • Children’s social workers and team managers who write referrals and profiles
  • Placement, sufficiency and commissioning teams who send them out
  • Foster carers, kinship carers and supervising social workers who read them
  • Registered managers and deputies in children’s homes who make admission decisions
  • Up to 20 people, with no prior training needed. Mixed groups of writers and readers work best

Session by session

Every part opens with a real story, adds the evidence behind it and ends with something people use the next day. Open a part to see what happens.

1The first read

A child’s profile is read next to the child’s own account of the same years, and the room notices the gap. Then what carers and homes look for in a referral, what puts them off and what is usually missing.

You leave with A clear picture of how your profiles are read on the other side

2Writing it

Why inherited case-note language sticks, and how to describe risk honestly without defining a child by it. Writers practise turning incident language into specific descriptions and adding strengths, interests and the child’s own words.

You leave with A profile template that holds risk, strengths and voice together

3Reading it

Carers and managers practise reading between the lines: what a recorded behaviour might mean for the child, what is missing, and which questions to ask the child’s social worker before deciding.

You leave with A reading checklist for any referral or profile

4Matching

Writers and readers work through a referral together, from first read to a matching decision, using Standards 11 and 15 of the Fostering National Minimum Standards and your own admissions process to decide what information is still needed.

You leave with A set of questions to ask before every matching decision

Questions commissioners ask

Should the people who write profiles and the people who read them come together?

It works best that way. Writers hear how their words are read, and carers and managers hear the pressures on the people writing. We can also run separate sessions for each group.

Does it cover fostering and residential matching?

Yes. It is for anyone who writes or reads referrals and profiles: children’s social workers, placement and commissioning teams, supervising social workers, foster carers and residential managers who make admission decisions.

How does it link to the standards?

It draws on the Fostering National Minimum Standards on the child’s wishes and feelings (Standard 1), preparation for a placement (Standard 11) and matching (Standard 15), and on the care planning standard in the Children’s Homes Regulations 2015. The original version, designed for Stockport’s fostering service in 2016, was mapped to five of the fostering standards.

Can we use our own referrals?

Yes, anonymised. Working on real referrals from your service makes the template and checklist fit your forms and your panel.

Can you shape it around our service?

Yes. We tailor the examples, the length and the mix of people in the room to your service, your procedures and the children you work with.

How do you price it?

We quote per cohort, based on how many people, the format and how much tailoring you want. Tell us what you need and we will send you a clear quote.
