Profiles that Find the Right Home: referral and profile trainingA profile is a first impression.

For the social workers and placement teams who write referrals and child profiles, and the foster carers, supervising social workers and residential managers who read them. Writers learn to set out risk honestly next to the child’s strengths, interests and own words. Readers learn to look for the child behind the incidents, so the yes or no is about the whole child.

Length
6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm. 2 hours live online
How
Face to face or live online
Group
Up to 60 people
For
Social workers, placement teams, foster carers and residential managers

The problem it solves

A referral or profile is often all a carer or home sees before saying yes or no, and the child is never in the room when it’s read. Many are built from inherited case notes and incident logs, so the child is described by what went wrong. The Fostering National Minimum Standards expect carers to receive all the information the service holds before a placement, and children’s home managers weigh each referral against the needs of the children already living there. When that information is mostly risk, good matches are missed and the child arrives already labelled. Each part of this course tells a child’s story twice, once by the paperwork and once by the child, explains why the gap matters, and ends with a template or checklist your team uses on the next referral.

Helps withStability and matchingRecording and the words we use

What changes

  • Write referrals that set out risk in context, alongside strengths, interests and the child’s own words
  • Replace labels inherited from old case notes with plain, specific descriptions of what happened
  • Read a profile for the child behind the incidents, and ask for what is missing before deciding
  • Use one shared profile template and reading checklist across the team
  • Are introduced to carers and homes as a whole person, not a list of incidents
  • See their own views, interests and achievements in what is written about them
  • Can read what was written about them later without feeling ashamed
  • Have a better chance of a home that is ready for them

Story

  • Describe how a child’s profile reads to a foster carer or registered manager deciding quickly, and what the same years look like from the child’s side.
  • Recognise inherited labels and incident language in a referral, and notice where the child’s own words, interests and strengths are missing.

Theory

  • Explain, using Solomon Asch’s research on first impressions (1946) and Michael White and David Epston’s idea of the problem-saturated story (1990), why what comes first and what is left out shapes a matching decision.
  • Describe what the Fostering National Minimum Standards (Standards 1, 11 and 15) and the Children’s Homes Regulations 2015 expect from information sharing and matching.

Action

  • Write a risk section that says what happened, how often, what helps and what has changed, with the child’s strengths, interests and views beside it, and a short profile the child could read.
  • Use the reading checklist and Separating Reason from Behaviour to spot what is missing from a referral and write the questions to ask before deciding.
  • Children’s social workers and team managers who write referrals and profiles
  • Placement, sufficiency and commissioning teams who send them out
  • Foster carers, kinship carers and supervising social workers who read them
  • Registered managers and deputies in children’s homes who make admission decisions
  • Up to 60 people, with no prior training needed. Mixed groups of writers and readers work best

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Every part tells a child’s story from both sides of the paperwork, gives you the theory for why the gap matters, and ends with a tool people practise on real referrals and use the next day. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1The first read

Story. Tia, 12, has a referral that opens with “history of aggression, absconding, damage to property”. The carer reading it has decided by the end of page one. Tia’s own account of the same year starts with her riding lessons and how much she wants to stay at her school.

Theory. Solomon Asch’s research on first impressions (1946) showed that what we read first colours how we read everything after it. The LUUT model, from Barnett Pearce and Vernon Cronen’s Coordinated Management of Meaning, separates the story that gets told from the story that was lived, and from the stories nobody heard or asked about.

Action. LUUT. Everyone reads Tia’s referral and writes down their decision and the first three things they remember. Then they read her own account and map what was told, what was lived and what went unheard. Readers say what made them hesitate, and writers hear it.

By the end of this part people can explain how the opening of a profile shapes a matching decision, and name what was missing from Tia’s.

You leave with A clear picture of how your profiles are read on the other side.

2Writing it

Story. Rhys, 15, has been described as “aggressive” in every referral since he was 11. It started with one note: he threw a cup when he was told he would not see his brother that weekend. Nobody has written that part since.

Theory. Michael White and David Epston (1990) called this a problem-saturated story: one thread repeated until it becomes the whole person. Dennis Saleebey’s strengths perspective (1992) asks what a child is good at, cares about and has got through, and puts that on the page beside the risk, not instead of it.

Action. Language that Cares and the profile template. Writers turn incident language into specific descriptions (what happened, when, how often, what helps, what has changed), then add strengths, interests and the child’s own words. Each writer drafts a short profile, from an anonymised case of their own, that the child could read.

By the end of this part people can write a risk section that is honest and specific without defining the child by it, and a short profile the child could read.

You leave with A profile template that holds risk, strengths and voice together.

3Reading it

Story. A registered manager reads that Kacey, 13, “goes missing”. There are no dates, no details and nothing about where she goes. Kacey goes to her nan’s, every time.

Theory. Behaviour is communication. A recorded behaviour tells you what an adult saw, not why it happened. Separating the behaviour from the reason, and reading for what’s missing as carefully as for what’s there, is what turns a referral into a fair decision.

Action. Separating Reason from Behaviour and the reading checklist. Carers and managers work through anonymised referrals, mark each behaviour, list possible reasons from the child’s side, note what is missing, and write the questions they would put to the child’s social worker before deciding.

By the end of this part people can read a referral for the child behind the incidents and write the questions to ask before saying yes or no.

You leave with A reading checklist for any referral or profile.

4Matching

Story. Arlo, 10, arrives at his new home at 9pm on a Friday. His referral didn’t say he’s frightened of the dark or that he only sleeps with his door open. His carer finds out at 2am.

Theory. Standards 11 (preparation for a placement) and 15 (matching) of the Fostering National Minimum Standards expect carers to get all the information the service holds before a child arrives. A children’s home manager has to weigh each referral against the needs of the children already living there. Neither works if the everyday details are missing.

Action. Writers and readers work through one referral together, from first read to a matching decision, using your own matching or admissions process. They agree what is still missing, including what the child needs on their first night, and turn it into a list of questions.

By the end of this part people can take a referral from first read to a matching decision together, and name the information still needed, including what a child needs on their first night.

You leave with A set of questions to ask before every matching decision.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the day structured?

Four parts, each built the same way. Story: a child’s story told twice, once by the paperwork and once by the child, such as Tia’s referral or the label Rhys has carried since he was 11. Theory: why first impressions and inherited labels shape matching, from Asch’s research on first impressions to White and Epston’s problem-saturated story, and what the standards expect. Action: a tool people use on the next referral: the profile template, the reading checklist and a set of matching questions. Face to face it is a 6-hour day, 10am to 4pm, covering all four parts. Live online it is one 2-hour session covering the key story, theory and tool from each part, with the workbook and on-demand course to go deeper.

Should the people who write profiles and the people who read them come together?

It works best that way. Writers hear how their words are read, and carers and managers hear the pressures on the people writing. We can also run separate sessions for each group.

Does it cover fostering and residential matching?

Yes. It is for anyone who writes or reads referrals and profiles: children’s social workers, placement and commissioning teams, supervising social workers, foster carers and residential managers who make admission decisions.

How does it link to the standards?

It draws on the Fostering National Minimum Standards on the child’s wishes and feelings (Standard 1), preparation for a placement (Standard 11) and matching (Standard 15), and on the care planning standard in the Children’s Homes Regulations 2015. The original version, designed for Stockport’s fostering service in 2016, was mapped to five of the fostering standards.

Can we use our own referrals?

Yes, anonymised. Working on real referrals from your service makes the template and checklist fit your forms and your panel.

How do you price it?

We quote per cohort, based on how many people, the format and how much tailoring you want. Tell us what you need and we will send you a clear quote.
