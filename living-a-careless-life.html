Living a Careless Life: care experience awareness trainingWhat it is really like to grow up in care.

Training that helps your team see their meetings, records and words through a child’s eyes. You leave with one change you will make within a week.

The problem it solves

Since the Children and Social Work Act 2017, councils have had to have regard to seven corporate parenting principles. One is taking children’s views, wishes and feelings into account. That is hard when most people in the room have never heard what care feels like from the inside. A review full of strangers. A case note written in incident language. A move announced at short notice. Each makes sense to the adult. Each tells the child whether they matter.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, people will be able to:

  1. 1

    Describe what a review, a placement move and a case note can feel like from a child’s side, using the stories told in the session.

  2. 2

    Identify the everyday moments in their own role where an adult builds or breaks a child’s trust.

  3. 3

    Explain how Bowlby’s attachment theory (1969), the window of tolerance (Dan Siegel, 1999) and the fundamental attribution error (Lee Ross, 1977) shape how adults read a child’s behaviour and file.

  4. 4

    Apply the seven corporate parenting principles in the Children and Social Work Act 2017 to a decision in their own role.

  5. 5

    Rewrite a line from a typical case note or profile using Language that Cares, so it describes the child as well as the incident.

  6. 6

    Plan one specific change to their own practice, to make within a week, and agree how their team will follow it up.

Our learning framework in this course

Every Care Leaders Learning course follows Story, Theory, Action. Here is how it runs through Living a Careless Life.

Story

Every part opens with a story from care.

In part 1, Luke tells his own story of growing up in care, alongside his work leading change in children’s services. Then composites: Ruby in her review, Callum’s incident log, and Ruby at 19.

Theory

Then the theory that explains what the child felt.

Bowlby’s attachment theory, the seven corporate parenting principles in the Children and Social Work Act 2017, TACT’s Language that Cares, Lee Ross’s fundamental attribution error and Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance.

Action

Then something you do differently the next working day.

A list of moments that built or broke trust, a checklist of system habits, three rewritten lines, a one-page evidence summary and one written commitment for the week.

The course in four parts

Every part opens with a story from care and ends with a tool people use the next day. When Luke Rodgers BEM delivers part 1, the story is his own, alongside his professional experience of leading change in children’s services. The other stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1The story

Story. When Luke Rodgers BEM delivers it, the story is his: he went into care at 10, was living in a B&B at 15 and in a caravan at 18, and gave his first keynote at 19. He tells the room what those years felt like from the inside, and which adults made a difference.

Theory. Bowlby’s attachment theory (1969) explains why one adult who stays, keeps their word and notices the small things can matter more to a child than any service. People listen for those adults in the story, and for the moments when nobody noticed.

Action. A trust timeline. People mark the moments in the story that built trust and the moments that broke it, then put one moment from their own working week alongside each.

By the end of this part people can describe what care felt like from one child’s side and name the adult actions that built or broke trust.

You leave with A list of the moments that built or broke trust, to hold up against your own practice

2The system

Story. Ruby, 15, sits in her review in a meeting room with nine adults, two of whom she has never met. She is asked how school is going in front of all of them. Her file later says she was “disengaged”.

Theory. The seven corporate parenting principles in the Children and Social Work Act 2017 say councils must encourage children to express their views and take those views into account. TACT’s Language that Cares (2019), written with young people in care, shows how the words adults inherit, such as “placement” and “contact”, can make a child feel like a case.

Action. Language that Cares. People read an anonymised profile next to the child’s own account of the same years, mark what the paperwork left out, and rewrite three lines so a child could read them without feeling like a case.

By the end of this part people can spot system habits in reviews, moves and records that leave a child out, and rewrite a line of a record so it describes the child as well as the incident.

You leave with A checklist of system habits to look for in your own meetings, moves and records, and three rewritten lines

3The evidence

Story. Callum, 13, laughs when his carer tells him off for breaking a door. The incident log says “shows no remorse”. The adults reading it a year later have never met him.

Theory. The fundamental attribution error (Lee Ross, 1977) explains why we put other people’s behaviour down to who they are, and our own down to the situation. Dan Siegel’s window of tolerance (1999) explains why a child under stress can laugh, freeze or go blank. Behaviour is communication: Callum’s laugh is more likely fear than a lack of feeling.

Action. Separating Reason from Behaviour. People take a line like “shows no remorse”, list three possible reasons from the child’s side, and write what a response to the reason would look like.

By the end of this part people can explain the fundamental attribution error and the window of tolerance in plain words, and use them to question one judgement in a child’s file.

You leave with A one-page summary of the evidence, written to share with colleagues

4The ask

Story. Ruby, now 19, remembers the one social worker who still texted her on her birthday after the case had closed. It took 30 seconds. It’s the thing she talks about first.

Theory. The corporate parenting principles again, this time as a test: does this decision act in the child’s best interests, take their views into account and help them feel safe and settled? Small, specific changes made by one person stick better than big promises made by a whole team.

Action. One person, one change, one week. Each person writes one specific thing they’ll do differently within a week. Leaders and elected members test a current decision against the seven principles. Teams take reflection questions back for their next team meeting or supervision.

By the end of this part people can test a real decision against the corporate parenting principles and commit to one change with a date to check it.

You leave with One written commitment to act on within a week, and reflection questions for your team

What changes

  • Spot the everyday moments, such as a cancelled visit or a new face at a review, that tell a child whether they matter
  • Speak and write about children in plain, warm language a child could read without feeling like a case
  • Respond to behaviour by asking what has happened to the child, not only what the child did
  • Take one specific change back to their team, with reflection questions for the next team meeting or supervision
  • Adults who explain what is happening and why, before it happens
  • Being spoken to and written about as a child, not a set of risks
  • At least one adult who goes a bit further for them
  • Being talked with, not talked about

Questions commissioners ask

How is the day structured?

Four parts, each built the same way. Story: a moment from care, starting with the facilitator’s own story and then composites such as Ruby in her review or Callum’s incident log. Theory: the idea that explains it, from attachment theory to the fundamental attribution error and the corporate parenting principles. Action: a tool people use in the room and take back, such as Language that Cares or Separating Reason from Behaviour. Face to face it runs for 6 hours, usually 10am to 4pm, with time to rewrite real records and plan changes as a team. Live online it is a 2-hour session covering the key story, theory and tool from each part.

Who delivers it?

People with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services, often Luke Rodgers BEM, who founded The Care Leaders and led the national engagement on England’s new fostering standards. They share their own experience alongside what the evidence says, then link both to your practice.

Does it work for elected members and corporate parenting boards?

Yes. The 2-hour live online session works well for boards and members. It shows what corporate parenting decisions look like from the child’s side and ends with each person testing a real decision against the seven corporate parenting principles.

What is the difference between face to face and live online?

Both use the same four parts. Live online gives the key story, theory and tool from each part in one 2-hour session on Teams or Zoom. Face to face is a 6-hour day that adds time for people to rewrite lines from their own records, look at their meetings and language, and agree as a team what they’ll change.

Is the story suitable for staff with their own experience of care?

The story is honest about hard moments without being graphic. Tell us when you book and we’ll share the themes in advance so you can brief colleagues.

Can you shape it around our service?

Yes. We tailor the examples and the mix of people in the room to your service, your procedures and the children you work with. For schools we focus on the classroom and the school day. For boards, on the decisions in front of them. If you send us an anonymised record or profile, we’ll use it in the rewriting activity.

How do you price it?

Each format has one flat fee. Face to face covers up to 60 people, and over 60 we charge for an additional facilitator. Live online covers up to 200 people. The workbook, the online course and the recording are included in the flat fee. Tell us what you need and we will send you a quote.
