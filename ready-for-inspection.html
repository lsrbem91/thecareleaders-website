Ready for Inspection: training for children’s home managersCan you show inspectors what children say?

A one-day course for registered managers, deputies and responsible individuals in children’s homes. You build everyday ways to hear what children think, record what changed as a result, and show it in children’s own words. Children get adults who act on what they say, and inspectors find the evidence on whatever day they arrive.

Length
6 hours face to face, usually 10am to 4pm. 2 hours live online
How
Face to face or live online
Group
Up to 60 people
For
Children’s home managers, responsible individuals and quality leads

The problem it solves

Regulation 7 of the Children’s Homes (England) Regulations 2015 expects staff to seek children’s views, explain how those views were considered and give reasons for decisions. Many homes do the asking but not the recording: views sit in a survey or a house meeting note, with nothing to show what happened next. Ofsted’s 2026 consultation on social care inspection proposes more consistent ways for inspectors to hear children’s lived experiences directly, so that gap will show. This course starts each part with what it feels like to be the child being asked, gives you the regulation or model that explains it, and ends with a tool you use in your home the next day.

What changes

  • Build hearing children into daily routines, key work and handovers, not only surveys and house meetings.
  • Record each view alongside what was done about it and why, as Regulation 7 expects, and tell the child the outcome, including when the answer is no.
  • Use children’s own words in Regulation 45 reviews and in responses to Regulation 44 reports.
  • Explain to children what an inspection is and who they can talk to, without telling them what to say.
  • Adults who ask what they think during ordinary days, in ways that suit them
  • Hearing back what happened after they spoke up, including the reason when the answer is no
  • Knowing how to complain and how to reach an advocate
  • Knowing what an inspector is there for, and feeling free to say what they actually think

Story

  • Describe what it is like to give your views in a home and never hear back, and how that changes what a child says next time.
  • Recognise the everyday moments in their own home, in the car, over food, at bedtime, when children say what they really think.

Theory

  • Explain what Regulation 7, the children’s views, wishes and feelings standard, requires of staff, how inspectors hear from children under the current SCCIF, and what Ofsted’s 2026 consultation proposes to change.
  • Use Laura Lundy’s model of participation (2007), space, voice, audience and influence, to test whether a child’s view in their home leads to anything.

Action

  • Write a record that links what a child said, what the home did and what changed, in the child’s own words, and use it as evidence in a Regulation 45 review.
  • Plan how to explain an inspection visit to the children in their home without coaching what they say.
  • Registered managers and deputy managers of children’s homes.
  • Responsible individuals, heads of service and quality assurance leads.
  • Regulation 44 independent persons, who review the same evidence.
  • Up to 60 people. Managers from several homes can join the same group.
  • People need to know their home’s recording system. No other preparation is needed, apart from bringing one anonymised log entry.

Session by session

  • Story
  • Theory
  • Action

Every part opens with a story from the child’s side, gives you the regulation or model that explains it and ends with a tool managers use on their own home’s records. The stories are composites. They draw on real experiences of care but are not one person. Open a part to see what happens.

1What changes

Story. Tasha, 14, fills in the house survey every month. She asked for a later bedtime at weekends, then for a say in the menu. Nobody told her what happened to either. By the third month she ticks the boxes without reading them.

Theory. Regulation 7 asks for more than a survey: staff seek children’s views, show how they were considered and give reasons for what was decided. Roger Hart’s ladder of participation (1992) names what happened to Tasha: being asked for show, with no feedback, is tokenism. We set out how inspectors hear from children under the SCCIF now, and what Ofsted’s 2026 consultation proposes for children’s homes, including a new area on children’s enduring relationships.

Action. Managers list every way their home heard from children in the last month, then mark which ones led to a recorded change and which children were told the outcome.

By the end of this part people can explain what Regulation 7 requires in plain words, and point to where their own home asks children without closing the loop.

You leave with A one-page summary of what inspectors look for when they listen to children.

2Listening

Story. Aiden, 11, says “dunno” to every key work session. On the drive to football he tells his key worker he misses his little sister and wants to see her more.

Theory. Laura Lundy’s model of participation (2007) says a child needs space to speak, support to give their view, an audience who can act on it and influence over what happens next. Aiden had space in the car. Whether he had influence depends on what the adult does next.

Action. Managers take one child from their home and build a listening plan: where and when the child talks best, how they communicate, who in the team they trust and how their view reaches the person who can act. They test it against Lundy’s four steps.

By the end of this part people can name at least three ways of hearing a child that fit the child’s age and communication, and check each one against space, voice, audience and influence.

You leave with A listening plan for each child, matched to how they like to talk.

3Evidence

Story. Sophie, 16, asks to read her records. She finds “refused key work” written three times, and nothing about the long conversation she had with staff about her mum’s birthday.

Theory. The LUUT model, from Barnett Pearce and Vernon Cronen’s Coordinated Management of Meaning, separates the story that gets told from the story that was lived. Records tell one story about a child. If the child’s words are not in them, the inspector and the child will both read a story the child does not recognise. Regulation 7 is the test: what the child said, what was considered and what was decided.

Action. Managers rewrite an anonymised entry from their own home’s log in three parts: what the child said, in their words; what the home did; what changed or why it did not. They use Language that Cares to take out labels such as “refused” and “attention seeking”, then swap with a partner and test each other’s entry against Regulation 7.

By the end of this part people can write a record that links a child’s view to the action taken and the outcome, in the child’s own words and without labels.

You leave with A recording format that links a child’s view to action, and one rewritten entry from your home.

4Inspection

Story. Lewis, 13, is told the night before an inspection to “remember to say how happy you are here”. When the inspector asks him how things are, he says nothing at all.

Theory. Inspectors speak to children in private because what children say unprompted is the best evidence a home has. Coaching tells a child their real view is not wanted. We link this to the Regulation 45 review of quality of care, where children’s views should already be showing up every six months, not the week before a visit.

Action. Managers write a short, plain explanation of inspection for the children in their home: who is coming, why, and that children can say what they actually think. Then they plan where children’s views will appear in their next Regulation 45 review and set three actions for the next month.

By the end of this part people can explain an inspection to a child without coaching them, and show where children’s views feed into their Regulation 45 review.

You leave with The voice and evidence toolkit, with an action plan for your home.

Questions commissioners ask

How is the day structured?

Four parts across a 6-hour day face to face, or one 2-hour live session online. Each one starts with a story from the child’s side, such as Tasha giving up on the house survey or Aiden only talking in the car. Then the regulation or model that explains it, from Regulation 7 and the SCCIF to Laura Lundy’s model of participation. Then a tool managers use in the room on their own home’s material: a listening plan, a rewritten log entry and an action plan for their next Regulation 45 review.

Is this based on Ofsted’s new framework?

It is based on the current SCCIF and the Children’s Homes (England) Regulations 2015, which you are inspected against now. We also cover what Ofsted proposed in its 2026 consultation. Ofsted plans to respond in early 2027 and bring changes in between spring 2027 and spring 2028, so we update the course as the final framework is published.

Will this help children perform well for inspectors?

No, and it should not. The course is about hearing children every day so inspectors see the real picture. We cover how to prepare children for a visit without coaching what they say.

Can you shape it around our service?

Yes. We use examples from your homes, your recording system and the children you care for. If you run a different kind of service, tell us and we will say honestly whether it fits.

Who delivers it?

People with lived experience of care and professional experience of children’s services. Every course is designed by Luke Rodgers BEM and follows the same Story, Theory, Action approach.

How do you price it?

We quote per cohort, based on how many people, the format and how much tailoring you want. Tell us what you need and we will send you a clear quote.
